#### D-05.1 Data Minimisation

<!-- participants: GDPR, CRA, AI_Act -->

**Participants (from CRDA):** GDPR, CRA, AI_Act

**Sole-authority note:** NIS2 and DORA are out-of-scope for D-05.1 per AEGIS taxonomy §4.1 — both are operational-resilience / incident-reporting focused, not data-lifecycle. GDPR is the dedicated authority; CRA addresses product-level (which can include non-personal data); AI Act addresses training-data-quality specifically.

##### Pair: GDPR ↔ CRA

**GDPR article (verbatim, Art. 5(1)(c) + Art. 6(4)):**
> "Personal data shall be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed ('data minimisation')." — Art. 5(1)(c) GDPR
>
> "Where the processing for a purpose other than that for which the personal data have been collected is not based on the data subject's consent … the controller shall, in order to ascertain whether processing for another purpose is compatible with the purpose for which the personal data are initially collected, take into account, inter alia: (a) any link between the purposes … (b) the context of collection … (c) the nature of the personal data … (d) the possible consequences … (e) the existence of appropriate safeguards." — Art. 6(4) GDPR

**CRA article (verbatim, Annex I Part I (2)(g) + Art. 13(3) sentence 2):**
> "On the basis of the cybersecurity risk assessment referred to in Article 13(2) … the product with digital elements shall: … (g) limit its attack surfaces, including attack surfaces of third-party components integrated into the product, by means of an internal architecture that minimises the exposure of the product's attack surfaces and the data processed by it, including by means of the processing of only the data that are adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the intended purpose of the product ('data minimisation')." — CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g)
>
> "The risk assessment shall be performed by the manufacturer and shall … take into account … the intended purpose of the product with digital elements, as well as the circumstances of use … including the reasonably foreseeable use thereof." — CRA Art. 13(3) sentence 2

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: personal data, processed by a controller / processor, calibrated to the controller's specified purposes.
- CRA scope: data processed by a product with digital elements (personal or other — Art. 3(1) product + Annex I (2)(g) generic data), calibrated to the product's intended purpose.
- Scope overlap: **Conditional (Y when the same dataset contains personal data and is processed by a CRA-classified product at runtime)**.

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: collection or further processing of personal data.
- CRA trigger: design / development of a product with digital elements with attack-surface implications.
- Trigger alignment: **DIFFERENT** — GDPR is triggered by data-processing events, CRA by product-design events. They overlap when the product's runtime data is personal data.

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: no quantitative threshold — proportionality is qualitative ("adequate, relevant, limited").
- CRA: no quantitative threshold — Annex I Part I (2)(g) is qualitative ("adequate, relevant, limited").
- Tension: **NONE (scope-conditional; thresholds align qualitatively)**.

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: supervisory authority (DPA), data subject.
- CRA: market surveillance authority (MSA), conformity-assessment body (notified body or self-assessment).

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: not codified — controller demonstrates Art. 5(2) accountability via documented necessity assessment (often via DPIA Art. 35).
- CRA: documented in Annex VII §3 risk-assessment record + Annex I Part I (2)(g) technical file.

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** COMPLEMENTARY
**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** **SAME — COMPLEMENTARY**
**Reasoning:** Both provisions use the identical operative language "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary" — verbatim wording overlap (GDPR Art. 5(1)(c); CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g)). CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g) explicitly extends GDPR's "data minimisation" terminology to products, anchored to the manufacturer-defined product purpose (Art. 3(23) `intended purpose`), while GDPR anchors to controller-defined purposes. When a CRA-compliant product processes personal data, both minimisation tests apply and the stricter operational interpretation binds. CRA does not require the Art. 6(4) compatibility test, so the residual GDPR-only requirement is the change-of-purpose assessment.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? **Conditional (Y when the same personal-data stream flows through a CRA-classified product at runtime)**. The two obligations stack rather than contradict.

**Downstream implication (Phase 1C Doc 07):** Phase 2 obligation derivation must produce a 2-track minimisation matrix: (1) GDPR Art. 5(1)(c) + Art. 6(4) personal-data minimisation with compatibility assessment; (2) CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g) product-data minimisation. For CRA-regulated products processing personal data, the union applies. The Art. 6(4) compatibility test is GDPR-only and must be retained as a residual obligation.

##### Pair: GDPR ↔ AI_Act

**GDPR article (verbatim, Art. 5(1)(c) + Art. 9(1)):**
> "Personal data shall be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed ('data minimisation')." — Art. 5(1)(c) GDPR
>
> "Processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person, data concerning health or data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation shall be prohibited." — Art. 9(1) GDPR

**AI_Act article (verbatim, Art. 10(2)(f)/(g) + Art. 10(5)):**
> "Training, validation and testing data sets shall be relevant, representative and, to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete. They shall have the appropriate statistical properties, including where applicable, as regards the persons or groups of persons on which the high-risk AI system is intended to be used. These properties of the data sets can be met at the level of individual data sets or a combination thereof … The data sets shall take into account, to the extent required by their intended purpose, the characteristics or elements that are particular to the specific geographical, behavioural or functional setting within which the high-risk AI system is intended to be used." — AI Act Art. 10(3)
>
> "Where it is not possible to fully comply with paragraph 4 in order to detect and prevent biases in relation to the training, validation and testing datasets of high-risk AI systems referred to in points (a) and (b) of Article 6(2), the providers of such systems may exceptionally process special categories of personal data referred to in Article 9(1) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 … insofar as the following conditions are fulfilled: (a) the bias detection and correction cannot be effectively achieved by processing other data; (b) the special categories of personal data are processed strictly necessary and limited to the bias detection and correction; (c) the providers have put in place effective technical and organisational safeguards to protect the data; (d) the datasets used for bias detection and correction are subject to enhanced data governance; (e) the safeguards are documented; (f) the special-category data are deleted after the bias detection and correction." — AI Act Art. 10(5)

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: any processing of special-category personal data is prohibited unless an Art. 9(2) basis applies.
- AI Act scope: training / validation / testing datasets of high-risk AI systems; processing of special-category data for bias detection / correction is *exceptionally* permitted when 6 conditions (a)–(f) are met.
- Scope overlap: **Y when training a high-risk AI system with bias-detection needs that cannot be met by other data.**

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: any processing of special-category personal data.
- AI Act trigger: bias detection and correction in training / validation / testing datasets of high-risk AI systems.
- Trigger alignment: **PARTIAL** — AI Act trigger is a *subset* of possible processing events (bias correction only); GDPR trigger is universal.

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: no quantitative threshold; prohibition unless Art. 9(2) applies.
- AI Act: 6 cumulative conditions (a)–(f) must all be satisfied.
- Tension: **GENUINE but regulated by Art. 10(5)** — the AI Act creates a carve-out into GDPR's prohibition. The carve-out does not contradict the principle of minimisation but creates a specific, narrowly-conditioned permission to process special-category data for bias detection.

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: data subject + supervisory authority (DPA).
- AI Act: provider + market surveillance authority (MSA) + national competent authorities for fundamental rights.

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: no codified template; controller documents Art. 9(2) basis and Art. 5(2) accountability.
- AI Act: providers must document the safeguards (condition (e)) and the bias-detection / correction process; data is deleted after the process (condition (f)).

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** CONTRADICTORY (special-category scope)
**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** **CORRECTED — SCOPE-DISJOINT** (with regulatory carve-out)
**Reasoning:** The CRDA reasoning itself acknowledges: "This is a regulatory carve-out *into* the data minimisation architecture, not a contradiction of minimisation as a principle, but it expands the set of permissible processing." That admission is dispositive. The AI Act Art. 10(5) provision is a *permission* that GDPR does not provide, conditioned on 6 safeguards. It is not a contradiction of GDPR Art. 5(1)(c) — it is an exception that allows *one specific additional purpose* (bias correction) for *one specific data class* (high-risk AI training data) when *one specific technical condition* (no alternative data) is met. The CRDA label "contradictory" is scope-incorrect; the correct label is "scope-disjoint" with explicit recognition of the Art. 10(5) carve-out. Two regulators cannot impose mutually exclusive obligations on the same event when one creates a conditional exception to the other — that is the textbook exception-permission pattern, not the prohibition-vs-prohibition pattern that "contradictory" connotes.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? **Y only for the narrow Art. 10(5) carve-out scenario: a high-risk AI provider training a system with bias-detection needs that cannot be met by non-special-category data.** Outside that narrow window, the AI Act does not trigger special-category processing, so the pair does not overlap. The pair is therefore scope-disjoint in baseline, with the carve-out as the single overlap point.

**Downstream implication (Phase 1C Doc 07):** Phase 2 obligation derivation must codify AI Act Art. 10(5) as a **gateway clause** that *expands* the set of permissible special-category processing under GDPR Art. 9(1) when all 6 conditions are satisfied. The CRDA "contradictory" label should be corrected to "scope-disjoint" with carve-out annotation. Phase 3 case generation must surface the 6-condition matrix explicitly when the case company is a high-risk AI provider processing special-category data for bias correction.

**Scope comparison:**
- CRA scope: data processed by the product at *runtime* (the product's runtime attack surface).
- AI Act scope: data processed by the *training pipeline* (upstream, lifecycle-stage-specific).
- Scope overlap: **N (typically)** — they operate at different lifecycle stages. The overlap is when AI Act training data flows into a CRA-classified AI-enabled product at runtime, in which case both apply in sequence.

**Trigger comparison:**
- CRA trigger: design / development of a product with digital elements with attack-surface implications.
- AI Act trigger: training / validation / testing of high-risk AI systems.
- Trigger alignment: **DIFFERENT** — upstream (training) vs runtime (execution).

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- CRA: no quantitative threshold (qualitative proportionality).
- AI Act: dataset quality criteria "to the best extent possible" — qualitative with explicit statistical dimension.
- Tension: **NONE**.

**Recipient comparison:**
- CRA: market surveillance authority (MSA).
- AI Act: provider + national competent authority + AI Office (post-market phase).

**Content template comparison:**
- CRA: documented in Annex VII §3 risk-assessment record.
- AI Act: dataset quality criteria documented per Art. 10(5) safeguards + Art. 11 technical documentation + Annex IV.

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** COMPLEMENTARY
**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** **SAME — COMPLEMENTARY**
**Reasoning:** CRA minimisation operates at runtime on data the product processes when in use; AI Act Art. 10 quality criteria operate upstream on the training pipeline. They address different data sets at different lifecycle stages. When the same dataset flows through both (training data for a CRA-classified high-risk AI product), the AI Act quality criteria sharpen the CRA runtime minimisation principle. No contradiction; full coverage requires both.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? **N (typically)**. They are layered at different lifecycle stages. The only overlap is at the data-flow boundary (training data → runtime data) which is sequential, not concurrent.

**Downstream implication (Phase 1C Doc 07):** Phase 2 obligation derivation must produce a 3-track minimisation matrix for AI-enabled CRA products (runtime minimisation + personal-data minimisation + training-data quality). Phase 3 case generation must validate that the runtime product does not retain more data than the training-data quality permits to flow into it.

**Objective.** Data collected or otherwise acquired is limited to what is adequate, relevant, and necessary in relation to the purpose for which it is processed, with a documented necessity assessment and, where the underlying purpose changes, a compatibility assessment confirming that the new use is consistent with the original. Special-category data is processed only where a narrow exception applies and is documented to that effect.

**Considerations.**
- Three participating regulations (GDPR, CRA, AI Act) each impose this duty on a distinct data class at a distinct trigger: GDPR Art. 5(1)(c) "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary" on personal data (controller-driven) plus the Art. 6(4) change-of-purpose compatibility test and Art. 9(1) special-category prohibition; CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g) verbatim "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary" on product data (manufacturer-design, tied to Art. 3(23) intended purpose); AI Act Art. 10(1)/(2) 8-element data-governance practice + Art. 10(3) 4-criterion dataset quality on training data (provider-driven).
- CRDA-deep verified relationships: GDPR↔CRA = SAME (verbatim operative-language match, controller-driven purpose vs manufacturer-design intended purpose); CRA↔AI_Act = SAME (runtime product data vs training pipeline data, different lifecycle stages); GDPR↔AI_Act = CORRECTED from CONTRADICTORY to SCOPE-DISJOINT with explicit AI Act Art. 10(5) carve-out into GDPR Art. 9(1) (the exception-permission pattern, not prohibition-vs-prohibition).
- The 1 conditional emergent tension (Art. 10(5) special-category gateway) is regulated by the 6-cumulative-condition matrix (a)–(f): bias detection cannot be achieved by other data + strict necessity + safeguards + enhanced governance + documentation + post-bias deletion. Outside that narrow window, GDPR Art. 9(1) prohibition binds and AI Act Art. 10(5) does not extend.
- Implementation guidance: the three data classes are layered not collapsed — personal data is collected from data subjects (GDPR), product data is collected at runtime by the product (CRA), training data is collected upstream for the training pipeline (AI Act). A unified data-management platform may serve the technical substrate, but policy and evidence layers must remain class-segregated.
- Scope caveats: GDPR-CRA verbatim language overlap is literal and the stricter operational interpretation binds on the shared risk substrate; CRA-AI_Act operates at sequential lifecycle stages (runtime vs training pipeline, disjoint in baseline); AI Act Art. 10(5) gateway does NOT extend to other special-category processing beyond the bias-correction window; the high-level SO does NOT relax GDPR Art. 9(1) prohibition beyond what Art. 10(5) explicitly permits.

#### Scope-overlap matrix (3×3)

#### Scope-overlap principle (operational). The high-level SO
(`SO-D-05.1.HL`) engages on a given party **if and only if ≥2 applicable
regulations' data-class scope-determiners overlap on the same party**
(e.g. a GDPR controller that also manufactures CRA-regulated products
that process personal data and operates a high-risk AI system trained
on special-category data for bias correction — a vertically-integrated
manufacturer-controller-provider on a multi-class data minimisation
architecture). Pure co-applicability of disjoint-data-class regulations
on different data objects does **not** trigger cross-regulation
aggregation — see the Activation logic in the file header. For a 5-FTE
SaaS startup with GDPR + CRA applicability but no AI Act applicability,
only the GDPR and CRA sub-SOs activate; the high-level SO engages on the
same party if both apply, with the boundary between CRA
product-data minimisation and GDPR personal-data minimisation
documented as the controlled hand-off (CRA-product minimisation
reduces the personal-data footprint at runtime; controller still owns
the upstream personal-data collection decision). **The three data
classes are layered, not collapsed**: personal data is collected from
data subjects (GDPR); product data is collected by the product at
runtime (CRA); training data is collected upstream for the training
pipeline (AI Act). The high-level SO surfaces the common
data-minimisation duty but does **NOT** aggregate the per-reg data
class, trigger, threshold anchor, lifecycle stage, recipient, or
evidence template.

#### Aggregation logic (from CRDA-deep verified classifications)

- 3 of 5 regulations participate (GDPR, CRA, AI_Act; NIS 2 and DORA
  are out-of-scope per AEGIS taxonomy §4.1 — both are operational-
  resilience/incident-reporting focused, not data-lifecycle; GDPR is
  the dedicated authority for personal-data minimisation; CRA
  addresses product-level data; AI Act addresses training-data quality
  and the special-category bias-correction exception).
- 3 pairwise cells, verified by CRDA-deep as **2 SAME + 1 CORRECTED**:
  GDPR↔CRA (SAME — COMPLEMENTARY, CRDA initial classification held);
  CRA↔AI_Act (SAME — COMPLEMENTARY, CRDA initial classification held);
  GDPR↔AI_Act (**CORRECTED** from CONTRADICTORY to SCOPE-DISJOINT
  with explicit Art. 10(5) carve-out — the AI Act creates a
  conditional exception to GDPR Art. 9(1) conditioned on 6 cumulative
  safeguards (a)–(f), which is the exception-permission pattern not
  the prohibition-vs-prohibition pattern; the CRDA reasoning itself
  concedes the carve-out framing, making the `CONTRADICTORY` label
  scope-incorrect).
- The three regulations impose the **same substantive data-minimisation
  duty** but each operates on a **distinct data class** at a **distinct
  trigger**: personal data (GDPR, controller-driven collection and
  further processing); product data (CRA, manufacturer-design at
  design-time and runtime); training data (AI Act, provider-driven
  training pipeline upstream of runtime). The threshold anchors align
  qualitatively (GDPR `adequate, relevant, necessary`; CRA `adequate,
  relevant, necessary in relation to intended purpose`; AI Act
  `relevant, sufficiently representative, free of errors, complete`)
  but the data classes, the triggers, and the evidence templates
  diverge. The verbatim "adequate, relevant, necessary" wording of
  GDPR and CRA makes those two a literal-linguistic match (R1 in the
  ambiguity treatment); AI Act uses a parallel-but-distinct 4-criterion
  quality formulation.
- The high-level SO describes the **COMMON OBJECTIVE** (limit data to
  what is adequate/relevant/necessary for the declared purpose;
  document the necessity assessment; apply the Art. 6(4) compatibility
  test on any change of purpose; honour the Art. 9(1) special-category
  prohibition absent an Art. 9(2) basis or the Art. 10(5) AI-Act
  gateway; deliver the evidence base); the **per-regulation
  perspective** (data class, trigger, anchor, lifecycle stage,
  evidence template) is preserved verbatim in the three sub-SOs.
- The high-level SO does **NOT** resolve the 1 conditional emergent
  tension — the **3-way interaction on special-category data** (GDPR
  Art. 9(1) prohibition; AI Act Art. 10(5) gateway; CRA Annex I
  Part I (2)(g) silent on special-category — only adequacy/relevance/
  necessity). The pairwise readings (GDPR↔CRA, GDPR↔AI_Act,
  CRA↔AI_Act) each capture part of the interaction, but the
  *non-pairwise observation* is that AI Act's Art. 10(5) exception
  *enables* a processing class (special-category data for bias
  correction) that neither GDPR nor CRA explicitly contemplates. The
  high-level SO codifies the AI Act Art. 10(5) exception as a
  **gateway clause**: it is *permitted* by AI Act, *limited* by GDPR
  Art. 9(2) residual constraints (where applicable — the Art. 10(5)
  conditions are the operative safeguard; the GDPR Art. 9(2) bases
  continue to apply in parallel per the Art. 10(5) `subject to
  appropriate safeguards` anchor), and *unspecified* by CRA (CRA
  defers to whichever regulation governs the personal-data status of
  the data). The tension is **conditional** (it engages only in the
  narrow Art. 10(5) window — high-risk AI provider training a system
  with bias-detection needs that cannot be met by non-special-category
  data, all 6 conditions (a)–(f) satisfied) and is **regulated** by
  the Art. 10(5) 6-condition matrix. Phase 1C Doc 07 documents the
  gateway-clause architecture; Phase 3 case generation must surface
  the 6-condition matrix explicitly when the case company is a
  high-risk AI provider processing special-category data for bias
  correction.
- **Scope caveat (CRITICAL — data-class divergence)** — derived
  from CRDA-deep verified classifications: the high-level SO does NOT
  collapse the three data classes into a single data-collection
  architecture. Personal data, product data, and training data are
  **distinct data objects** at **distinct layers** (collection from
  data subjects vs runtime product capture vs upstream training
  pipeline), with distinct triggers, distinct threshold anchors, and
  distinct evidence templates (Art. 5(2) accountability + Art. 35
  DPIA + Art. 30 RoPA vs Annex VII §2 technical documentation +
  Annex VII §3 risk assessment vs Annex IV technical documentation +
  Art. 10(2) data-governance practice records + Art. 10(5) safeguard
  records). A unified data-management platform may serve the technical
  substrate (e.g. a unified data catalog with multiple data-class
  views), but the policy and evidence layers must remain
  **class-segregated** for compliance demonstration. Phase 1A must
  therefore emit **three checks before applying the high-level SO**
  (one per participating regulation) to confirm that the per-reg
  data class, trigger, threshold anchor, lifecycle stage, recipient,
  and evidence template are documented separately; the high-level SO
  surfaces the common data-minimisation duty but does NOT aggregate
  the per-reg data classes.
- **Scope caveat (CRITICAL — GDPR-CRA verbatim language overlap is
  literal)** — derived from CRDA-deep verified classifications: GDPR
  Art. 5(1)(c) and CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g) use the **verbatim
  operative language** "adequate, relevant and limited to what is
  necessary". The wording overlap is literal and unambiguous. For
  a party in both GDPR and CRA scope, the same data stream flowing
  through a CRA-classified product at runtime satisfies both
  minimisation tests on the same operative phrase. CRA does NOT
  require the Art. 6(4) compatibility test; the **residual
  GDPR-only requirement** is the change-of-purpose compatibility
  assessment. The controller-side responsibility for the Art. 6(4)
  compatibility test is not absorbed by the CRA-side design-time
  minimisation. For an integrated-stack manufacturer-controller (the
  same party is both manufacturer of the CRA product and controller
  of the personal data processing), the manufacturer-controller must
  document the Art. 6(4) compatibility assessment separately from
  the CRA-side Annex I Part I (2)(g) design-time minimisation.
- **Scope caveat (CRITICAL — CRA-AI_Act lifecycle-stage
  divergence)** — derived from CRDA-deep verified classifications:
  CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g) operates on data the product processes
  **at runtime**; AI Act Art. 10 operates on data the **training
  pipeline** uses upstream. They address different data sets at
  different lifecycle stages. When the same dataset flows through
  both (training data for a CRA-classified high-risk AI product),
  the AI Act training-stage quality criteria (relevant, representative,
  free of errors, complete) sharpen the CRA runtime minimisation
  principle (adequate, relevant, necessary for intended purpose).
  For an AI-enabled CRA product, the data-minimisation architecture
  must be a **3-track matrix** (CRA runtime product data minimisation
  + GDPR personal-data minimisation for the personal-data subset +
  AI Act training-data quality for the upstream training pipeline).
  Phase 3 case generation must validate that the runtime product does
  not retain more data than the training-data quality permits to flow
  into it (the upstream→downstream minimisation hand-off).
- **Scope caveat (CRITICAL — AI_Act Art. 10(5) gateway)** — derived
  from CRDA-deep verified classifications: the AI Act Art. 10(5)
  special-category exception is the **only point where data
  minimisation is *relaxed*** by one of the three regulations. The
  relaxation is **conditional** on 6 cumulative conditions (a)–(f)
  all required: (a) the bias detection and correction cannot be
  effectively achieved by processing other data; (b) the special
  categories of personal data are processed strictly necessary and
  limited to the bias detection and correction; (c) the providers
  have put in place effective technical and organisational safeguards
  to protect the data; (d) the datasets used for bias detection and
  correction are subject to enhanced data governance; (e) the
  safeguards are documented; (f) the special-category data are
  deleted after the bias detection and correction. Outside this
  narrow window, the GDPR Art. 9(1) prohibition binds; the
  exception-permission pattern does not extend to other
  special-category processing. The high-level SO does NOT relax the
  GDPR Art. 9(1) prohibition beyond what Art. 10(5) explicitly
  permits; the residual GDPR Art. 9(2) bases continue to apply in
  parallel.

**Why contradictory (special-category scope)**: GDPR Art. 9(1) prohibits special-category personal data processing absent one of Art. 9(2) bases; AI Act Art. 10(5) creates an *exception* to GDPR Art. 9(1) — providers of high-risk AI may exceptionally process special-category data for bias detection/correction, subject to 6 cumulative conditions. This is a regulatory carve-out *into* the data minimisation architecture, not a contradiction of minimisation as a principle, but it expands the set of permissible processing. Phase 2 must surface this exception explicitly and codify that the AI Act exception applies only when all 6 conditions (a)–(f) are simultaneously satisfied.

**Why complementary**: CRA minimisation is about data the *product* processes at runtime; AI Act data governance is about data the *training pipeline* uses upstream. They operate at different lifecycle stages: CRA = runtime, AI Act = training. When the same data set flows through both (training data for a CRA-classified high-risk AI product), both minimisations apply in sequence. AI Act's training-data-quality criteria (relevant, representative, free of errors, complete) are a training-stage sharpening of CRA's runtime minimisation principle.

##### Emergent tensions (3+ regulations)
<!-- emergent: GDPR,CRA,AI_Act -->

- **Special-category-data exception creates a 3-way interaction.** GDPR Art. 9(1) prohibits, AI Act Art. 10(5) creates the exception, CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g) addresses only adequacy/relevance/necessity (silent on special-category). The pairwise readings (GDPR↔CRA, GDPR↔AI_Act, CRA↔AI_Act) each capture part of the interaction, but the *non-pairwise observation* is that AI Act's exception enables a processing class that neither GDPR nor CRA explicitly contemplates. Phase 2 must codify the AI Act exception as a gateway clause: it is *permitted* by AI Act, *limited* by GDPR Art. 9(2) residual constraints, and *unspecified* by CRA (CRA defers to whichever regulation governs the personal-data status of the data).

<!-- /emergent -->

##### SR cross-validation
NIST CSF 2.0 anchoring is dominated by **PR.DS-12** (Data disposal managed) and **ID.AM-03** (Asset inventory prioritised). GDPR SR-GDPR-025 maps both directly. CRA SR-CRA-046 maps PR.DS-12 + ID.AM-03. AI Act SR-AIACT-004/005 adds PR.DS-01 (Data-at-rest protected) and PR.PS-06 (Secure software development practices integrated). The CSF 2.0 surface is consistent — no CSF cross-mapping conflict. The CSF layer does not separately enumerate training-data minimisation, which is a known gap; AI Act's data-governance obligation is partially outside the CSF 2.0 scope and is appropriately flagged as a regulatory-specific construct.

##### Downstream implication
Phase 2 obligation derivation must produce a 3-track minimisation matrix: (1) runtime minimisation (CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g)), (2) personal-data minimisation (GDPR Art. 5(1)(c) + Art. 6(4)), (3) training-data quality (AI Act Art. 10(1)–(5)). For high-risk AI products processing personal data, all three apply simultaneously. Phase 3 case generation must surface the AI Act Art. 10(5) special-category exception explicitly, because it is the only point where data minimisation is *relaxed* by one of the three regulations.

#### D-05.1 Data Minimisation

<!-- participants: GDPR, CRA, AI_Act -->

**Participants (from CRDA):** GDPR, CRA, AI_Act

**Sole-authority note:** NIS2 and DORA are out-of-scope for D-05.1 per AEGIS taxonomy §4.1 — both are operational-resilience / incident-reporting focused, not data-lifecycle. GDPR is the dedicated authority; CRA addresses product-level (which can include non-personal data); AI Act addresses training-data-quality specifically.

##### Pair: GDPR ↔ CRA

**GDPR article (verbatim, Art. 5(1)(c) + Art. 6(4)):**
> "Personal data shall be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed ('data minimisation')." — Art. 5(1)(c) GDPR
>
> "Where the processing for a purpose other than that for which the personal data have been collected is not based on the data subject's consent … the controller shall, in order to ascertain whether processing for another purpose is compatible with the purpose for which the personal data are initially collected, take into account, inter alia: (a) any link between the purposes … (b) the context of collection … (c) the nature of the personal data … (d) the possible consequences … (e) the existence of appropriate safeguards." — Art. 6(4) GDPR

**CRA article (verbatim, Annex I Part I (2)(g) + Art. 13(3) sentence 2):**
> "On the basis of the cybersecurity risk assessment referred to in Article 13(2) … the product with digital elements shall: … (g) limit its attack surfaces, including attack surfaces of third-party components integrated into the product, by means of an internal architecture that minimises the exposure of the product's attack surfaces and the data processed by it, including by means of the processing of only the data that are adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the intended purpose of the product ('data minimisation')." — CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g)
>
> "The risk assessment shall be performed by the manufacturer and shall … take into account … the intended purpose of the product with digital elements, as well as the circumstances of use … including the reasonably foreseeable use thereof." — CRA Art. 13(3) sentence 2

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: personal data, processed by a controller / processor, calibrated to the controller's specified purposes.
- CRA scope: data processed by a product with digital elements (personal or other — Art. 3(1) product + Annex I (2)(g) generic data), calibrated to the product's intended purpose.
- Scope overlap: **Conditional (Y when the same dataset contains personal data and is processed by a CRA-classified product at runtime)**.

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: collection or further processing of personal data.
- CRA trigger: design / development of a product with digital elements with attack-surface implications.
- Trigger alignment: **DIFFERENT** — GDPR is triggered by data-processing events, CRA by product-design events. They overlap when the product's runtime data is personal data.

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: no quantitative threshold — proportionality is qualitative ("adequate, relevant, limited").
- CRA: no quantitative threshold — Annex I Part I (2)(g) is qualitative ("adequate, relevant, limited").
- Tension: **NONE (scope-conditional; thresholds align qualitatively)**.

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: supervisory authority (DPA), data subject.
- CRA: market surveillance authority (MSA), conformity-assessment body (notified body or self-assessment).

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: not codified — controller demonstrates Art. 5(2) accountability via documented necessity assessment (often via DPIA Art. 35).
- CRA: documented in Annex VII §3 risk-assessment record + Annex I Part I (2)(g) technical file.

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** COMPLEMENTARY
**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** **SAME — COMPLEMENTARY**
**Reasoning:** Both provisions use the identical operative language "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary" — verbatim wording overlap (GDPR Art. 5(1)(c); CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g)). CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g) explicitly extends GDPR's "data minimisation" terminology to products, anchored to the manufacturer-defined product purpose (Art. 3(23) `intended purpose`), while GDPR anchors to controller-defined purposes. When a CRA-compliant product processes personal data, both minimisation tests apply and the stricter operational interpretation binds. CRA does not require the Art. 6(4) compatibility test, so the residual GDPR-only requirement is the change-of-purpose assessment.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? **Conditional (Y when the same personal-data stream flows through a CRA-classified product at runtime)**. The two obligations stack rather than contradict.

**Downstream implication (Phase 1C Doc 07):** Phase 2 obligation derivation must produce a 2-track minimisation matrix: (1) GDPR Art. 5(1)(c) + Art. 6(4) personal-data minimisation with compatibility assessment; (2) CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g) product-data minimisation. For CRA-regulated products processing personal data, the union applies. The Art. 6(4) compatibility test is GDPR-only and must be retained as a residual obligation.

##### Pair: GDPR ↔ AI_Act

**GDPR article (verbatim, Art. 5(1)(c) + Art. 9(1)):**
> "Personal data shall be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed ('data minimisation')." — Art. 5(1)(c) GDPR
>
> "Processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership, and the processing of genetic data, biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person, data concerning health or data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation shall be prohibited." — Art. 9(1) GDPR

**AI_Act article (verbatim, Art. 10(2)(f)/(g) + Art. 10(5)):**
> "Training, validation and testing data sets shall be relevant, representative and, to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete. They shall have the appropriate statistical properties, including where applicable, as regards the persons or groups of persons on which the high-risk AI system is intended to be used. These properties of the data sets can be met at the level of individual data sets or a combination thereof … The data sets shall take into account, to the extent required by their intended purpose, the characteristics or elements that are particular to the specific geographical, behavioural or functional setting within which the high-risk AI system is intended to be used." — AI Act Art. 10(3)
>
> "Where it is not possible to fully comply with paragraph 4 in order to detect and prevent biases in relation to the training, validation and testing datasets of high-risk AI systems referred to in points (a) and (b) of Article 6(2), the providers of such systems may exceptionally process special categories of personal data referred to in Article 9(1) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 … insofar as the following conditions are fulfilled: (a) the bias detection and correction cannot be effectively achieved by processing other data; (b) the special categories of personal data are processed strictly necessary and limited to the bias detection and correction; (c) the providers have put in place effective technical and organisational safeguards to protect the data; (d) the datasets used for bias detection and correction are subject to enhanced data governance; (e) the safeguards are documented; (f) the special-category data are deleted after the bias detection and correction." — AI Act Art. 10(5)

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: any processing of special-category personal data is prohibited unless an Art. 9(2) basis applies.
- AI Act scope: training / validation / testing datasets of high-risk AI systems; processing of special-category data for bias detection / correction is *exceptionally* permitted when 6 conditions (a)–(f) are met.
- Scope overlap: **Y when training a high-risk AI system with bias-detection needs that cannot be met by other data.**

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: any processing of special-category personal data.
- AI Act trigger: bias detection and correction in training / validation / testing datasets of high-risk AI systems.
- Trigger alignment: **PARTIAL** — AI Act trigger is a *subset* of possible processing events (bias correction only); GDPR trigger is universal.

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: no quantitative threshold; prohibition unless Art. 9(2) applies.
- AI Act: 6 cumulative conditions (a)–(f) must all be satisfied.
- Tension: **GENUINE but regulated by Art. 10(5)** — the AI Act creates a carve-out into GDPR's prohibition. The carve-out does not contradict the principle of minimisation but creates a specific, narrowly-conditioned permission to process special-category data for bias detection.

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: data subject + supervisory authority (DPA).
- AI Act: provider + market surveillance authority (MSA) + national competent authorities for fundamental rights.

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: no codified template; controller documents Art. 9(2) basis and Art. 5(2) accountability.
- AI Act: providers must document the safeguards (condition (e)) and the bias-detection / correction process; data is deleted after the process (condition (f)).

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** CONTRADICTORY (special-category scope)
**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** **CORRECTED — SCOPE-DISJOINT** (with regulatory carve-out)
**Reasoning:** The CRDA reasoning itself acknowledges: "This is a regulatory carve-out *into* the data minimisation architecture, not a contradiction of minimisation as a principle, but it expands the set of permissible processing." That admission is dispositive. The AI Act Art. 10(5) provision is a *permission* that GDPR does not provide, conditioned on 6 safeguards. It is not a contradiction of GDPR Art. 5(1)(c) — it is an exception that allows *one specific additional purpose* (bias correction) for *one specific data class* (high-risk AI training data) when *one specific technical condition* (no alternative data) is met. The CRDA label "contradictory" is scope-incorrect; the correct label is "scope-disjoint" with explicit recognition of the Art. 10(5) carve-out. Two regulators cannot impose mutually exclusive obligations on the same event when one creates a conditional exception to the other — that is the textbook exception-permission pattern, not the prohibition-vs-prohibition pattern that "contradictory" connotes.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? **Y only for the narrow Art. 10(5) carve-out scenario: a high-risk AI provider training a system with bias-detection needs that cannot be met by non-special-category data.** Outside that narrow window, the AI Act does not trigger special-category processing, so the pair does not overlap. The pair is therefore scope-disjoint in baseline, with the carve-out as the single overlap point.

**Downstream implication (Phase 1C Doc 07):** Phase 2 obligation derivation must codify AI Act Art. 10(5) as a **gateway clause** that *expands* the set of permissible special-category processing under GDPR Art. 9(1) when all 6 conditions are satisfied. The CRDA "contradictory" label should be corrected to "scope-disjoint" with carve-out annotation. Phase 3 case generation must surface the 6-condition matrix explicitly when the case company is a high-risk AI provider processing special-category data for bias correction.

##### Pair: CRA ↔ AI_Act

**CRA article (verbatim, Annex I Part I (2)(g)):**
> "On the basis of the cybersecurity risk assessment referred to in Article 13(2) … the product with digital elements shall: … (g) limit its attack surfaces … by means of … the processing of only the data that are adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the intended purpose of the product ('data minimisation')." — CRA Annex I Part I (2)(g)

**AI_Act article (verbatim, Art. 10(1)–(3)):**
> "High-risk AI systems which make use of techniques involving the training of AI models with data shall be developed on the basis of training, validation and testing data sets that meet the quality criteria referred to in paragraphs 2 to 5 whenever such data sets are used." — AI Act Art. 10(1)
>
> "Training, validation and testing data sets shall be relevant, representative and, to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete." — AI Act Art. 10(3)

**Scope comparison:**
- CRA scope: data processed by the product at *runtime* (the product's runtime attack surface).
- AI Act scope: data processed by the *training pipeline* (upstream, lifecycle-stage-specific).
- Scope overlap: **N (typically)** — they operate at different lifecycle stages. The overlap is when AI Act training data flows into a CRA-classified AI-enabled product at runtime, in which case both apply in sequence.

**Trigger comparison:**
- CRA trigger: design / development of a product with digital elements with attack-surface implications.
- AI Act trigger: training / validation / testing of high-risk AI systems.
- Trigger alignment: **DIFFERENT** — upstream (training) vs runtime (execution).

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- CRA: no quantitative threshold (qualitative proportionality).
- AI Act: dataset quality criteria "to the best extent possible" — qualitative with explicit statistical dimension.
- Tension: **NONE**.

**Recipient comparison:**
- CRA: market surveillance authority (MSA).
- AI Act: provider + national competent authority + AI Office (post-market phase).

**Content template comparison:**
- CRA: documented in Annex VII §3 risk-assessment record.
- AI Act: dataset quality criteria documented per Art. 10(5) safeguards + Art. 11 technical documentation + Annex IV.

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** COMPLEMENTARY
**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** **SAME — COMPLEMENTARY**
**Reasoning:** CRA minimisation operates at runtime on data the product processes when in use; AI Act Art. 10 quality criteria operate upstream on the training pipeline. They address different data sets at different lifecycle stages. When the same dataset flows through both (training data for a CRA-classified high-risk AI product), the AI Act quality criteria sharpen the CRA runtime minimisation principle. No contradiction; full coverage requires both.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? **N (typically)**. They are layered at different lifecycle stages. The only overlap is at the data-flow boundary (training data → runtime data) which is sequential, not concurrent.

**Downstream implication (Phase 1C Doc 07):** Phase 2 obligation derivation must produce a 3-track minimisation matrix for AI-enabled CRA products (runtime minimisation + personal-data minimisation + training-data quality). Phase 3 case generation must validate that the runtime product does not retain more data than the training-data quality permits to flow into it.

### 4. Cross-clause structural observations (Art. 25)

1. **Art. 25 is encouragement-only.** No binding entity obligation. The article's operational impact is on the entity's evidence base: standards cited in Art. 25(1) and ENISA guidelines produced under Art. 25(2) are non-mandatory but provide a soft path to Art. 21 compliance.

2. **The technology-neutrality clause is the most distinctive single phrase.** `without imposing or discriminating in favour of the use of a particular type of technology` is the only explicit technology-neutrality mandate in Chapter IV. It is a structural commitment to standards-based (not technology-specific) regulation.

3. **ENISA's `shall draw up advice and guidelines` is one of the few mandatory provisions on ENISA in Chapter IV.** Most ENISA references in Chapter IV are `may` (Art. 21(5), Art. 22(1), Art. 23(11), Art. 24(3)). Art. 25(2) is `shall draw up` — a positive obligation.

4. **Cross-regulation parallel.** Art. 25 mirrors the standardisation provisions in CRA Art. 27 (presumption of conformity via harmonised standards) and AI Act Art. 40 (conformity assessment via harmonised standards). The `European and international standards and technical specifications` triplet is the standard cross-regulation formulation.

#### 6.3 Art. 6 chapeau — the framework's three-attribute triad

Art. 6(1) opens with `sound, comprehensive and well-documented` (three attributes, AND). This is the **framework-level analog** of:

- **Art. 24(1) `sound and comprehensive`** (testing programme, two attributes; CL24-1)
- **CRA Annex I Part I §1 chapeau** `products with digital elements shall be designed, developed and produced …` (manufacturing lifecycle, three verbs; CRA-CL14)
- **NIS 2 Art. 21(1) `appropriate and proportionate technical, operational and organisational measures`** (measures chapeau)

The 3-attribute triad (`sound, comprehensive and well-documented`) is **the most demanding framework chapeau in the 5-regulation corpus** — it requires three distinct qualities (effectiveness, completeness, traceability). CRA Annex I Part I §1 has three verbs (design, develop, produce) but they describe **processes** not qualities. DORA's triad describes **qualities of an artifact** (the framework itself), which is a different kind of obligation.

- **Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`sound`, `comprehensive`, `well-documented`); §5.4.7 (AND); §5.5 (parallelism).
- **Structural implication:** the framework's three attributes set the bar for every Ch. II–V Sec. I clause. Any clause that fails one of the three (e.g. a framework that is `comprehensive and well-documented` but not `sound`) would be non-compliant.
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** the triad is the most explicit framework-quality specification in the corpus. NIS 2 Art. 21(1) uses `appropriate and proportionate` (2 qualifiers); CRA Annex I Part I uses process verbs; GDPR Art. 24 uses `appropriate` (1 qualifier). DORA's 3-attribute triad is the most demanding.

#### 6.5 Art. 6(4) — the 3-lines-of-defence option

Art. 6(4) gives financial entities **two control-model options** (OR): the **three lines of defence** model (business → risk management → internal audit) OR **any internal risk management and control model**. The 3-lines-of-defence model is the **banking-supervision standard** (Basel Committee, EBA Guidelines on Internal Governance), but DORA Art. 6(4) explicitly **opens the door to alternative models** (e.g. COSO, ISO 31000, or proprietary models).

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`three lines of defence`); §5.4.7 (OR).
- **Structural implication:** the option is **non-trivial** because the 3-lines-of-defence model implies three distinct functions with separate reporting lines; alternative models may have two (business + control) or four (split internal audit from compliance). The model choice affects the segregation requirement in CL6-3 Instance 1-3.
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** the 3-lines-of-defence model is **specific to EU financial-services law**. NIS 2 has no equivalent. CRA has no equivalent. The closest analog is **CRD IV Art. 74** (internal governance arrangements) which uses similar language.

| (a) Initial notification | After awareness of major incident | RTS (Art. 20(a)(ii)) |
| (b) Intermediate report | Status change / new information / CA request | RTS (Art. 20(a)(ii)) |
| (c) Final report | Root cause analysis completed + actual impact figures available | RTS (Art. 20(a)(ii)) |

**None of these time limits live in OJ.** The literal Art. 19(4) chapeau says `within the time limits to be laid down in accordance with Article 20, first paragraph, point (a), point (ii)` — the OJ text references an RTS that was not in force at the time the regulation was adopted. As of mid-2025, **Reg. (EU) 2024/2956 Annex II** addresses the initial-notification content and Annex IV addresses the templates, but the per-tier time limits are layered with sectoral differentiation (Art. 20 chapeau: `different time limits may reflect, as appropriate, specificities of financial sectors`).

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`time limits`); §5.7 (temporal); §5.1 (`specificities`).
- **Structural implication:** the Ch. III timeline is **functionally identical to NIS 2 Art. 23(4)** (24h/72h/1-month triple tier) and **GDPR Art. 33** (72h notification) in spirit, but the **specific hour-anchors are sector-differentiated** rather than uniform. This is a deliberate DORA design choice (per recital 56: `duly consideration to any specific feature arising from the distinct nature of activities across different financial services sectors`).
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** the NIS 2 24h/72h/1-month trio is the **uniform template**; DORA adopts a **sector-differentiated template** that is more flexible but less predictable. **Layer 2 must surface this design divergence.**

#### 6.8 Art. 19 — voluntary cyber-threat notification vs. mandatory incident reporting

Ch. III distinguishes **mandatory** incident reporting (Art. 19(1), 19(4)) from **voluntary** cyber-threat notification (Art. 19(2)). The voluntary notification is at entity discretion and for significant cyber threats (DORA-D13, defined per Art. 18(2)).

The asymmetry is:

| Trigger | Mandatory? |
|---|---|
| Major ICT-related incident | **Yes** — Art. 19(1)+(4) |
| Significant cyber threat | **No** — Art. 19(2) voluntary |

This is **structurally different** from the NIS 2 model (where significant cyber threats are part of Art. 23(11) implementing-acts scope and Art. 23(2) voluntary cyber-threat communication to recipients only). DORA's Art. 19(2) is **CA-directed** (the entity notifies the CA, not the recipients); NIS 2's Art. 23(2) is **recipient-directed**.

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`may, on a voluntary basis`); §5.1 (`deem`).
- **Structural implication:** the asymmetry preserves entity discretion on **pre-incident** intelligence but mandates **post-incident** reporting.
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** the closest parallel is **NIS 2 Art. 23(2)** (recipients-communication of cyber threats) — but NIS 2 only requires entity-to-recipient communication; DORA Art. 19(2) only requires entity-to-CA communication. **The two are not strictly equivalent.** Layer 2 must adjudicate the divergence.

#### 4.6 CL25-1 — Art. 25(1) — Open-list test modalities

**Source locus:** Art. 25(1).

> The digital operational resilience testing programme referred to in Article 24 shall provide, in accordance with the criteria set out in Article 4(2), for the execution of **appropriate tests, such as vulnerability assessments and scans, open source analyses, network security assessments, gap analyses, physical security reviews, questionnaires and scanning software solutions, source code reviews where feasible, scenario-based tests, compatibility testing, performance testing, end-to-end testing and penetration testing**.

**Instance 1 — POLY — S3 — `appropriate tests, such as …` (open list of 12 modalities)**

The list is **explicitly open** (`such as …`) but enumerates 12 specific modalities. Three readings of how to treat the list:

- **R1:** Closed list (the 12 are exhaustive; financial entities must conduct all of them).
- **R2:** Open list (the 12 are illustrative; entities must choose from these or add others).
- **R3:** Open list with 12-floor (entities must conduct at least these 12).

| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Closed list | None in OJ |
| R2 | Open list | In-Directive: literal `such as` (Berry §5.1 open-list signal) |
| R3 | Open with floor | None in OJ |

R2 is the literal reading. R1 vs R2 admit materially distinct compliance populations (R1: entity conducts only the 12; R2: entity adds modalities e.g. red team, social engineering, fuzz testing). S3.

*Berry anchor:* §5.1 (`such as`); cf. CRA-C05 (Annex I Part I §2(d) `including but not limited to`).

**Instance 2 — POLY — S2 — 12 individual modalities**

Each modality admits internal ambiguity (e.g. `vulnerability assessments and scans` could mean automated-only, manual-only, or hybrid; `penetration testing` could be black-box, grey-box, or white-box).

| Modality | Key ambiguity | Berry anchor |
|---|---|---|
| `vulnerability assessments and scans` | Automated vs. manual scope | §3.3.1 (`assessments`) |
| `open source analyses` | OSS code review vs. OSS dependency analysis | §3.3.1 (`open source`) |
| `network security assessments` | External vs. internal network | §3.3.1 (`network`) |
| `gap analyses` | Compliance-gap vs. capability-gap | §3.3.1 (`gap`) |
| `physical security reviews` | Premises-only vs. hardware-inclusive | §3.3.1 (`physical security`) |
| `questionnaires and scanning software solutions` | Self-assessment vs. auditor-driven | §3.3.1 (`questionnaires`) |
| `source code reviews where feasible` | `feasible` undefined; static vs. dynamic | §5.1 (`feasible`) |
| `scenario-based tests` | Adversary-emulation vs. failure-mode | §3.3.1 (`scenario`) |
| `compatibility testing` | Interoperability vs. backward-compat | §3.3.1 (`compatibility`) |
| `performance testing` | Load vs. stress vs. soak | §3.3.1 (`performance`) |
| `end-to-end testing` | Business-process vs. data-flow | §3.3.1 (`end-to-end`) |
| `penetration testing` | Black-box vs. grey-box vs. white-box | §3.3.1 (`penetration`) |

Each row is S2 on its own (the modality is recognised; the specific implementation is open). The aggregate is the open-list pattern at S3.

**Definitional anchor:** DORA Art. 3(2) → NIS 2 Art. 6(1) `network and information system`; DORA-D17 `TLPT` (for the penetration-testing sub-modality when elevated to TLPT, see CL26-*).
**Proportionality-status:** Art. 4 applies.

#### 4.7 CL25-2 — Art. 25(2) — CSD/CCP pre-deployment vulnerability assessments

**Source locus:** Art. 25(2).

> Central securities depositories and central counterparties shall perform vulnerability assessments **before any deployment or redeployment of new or existing applications and infrastructure components, and ICT services supporting critical or important functions** of the financial entity.

**Instance 1 — POLY — S2 — `deployment or redeployment`**

`deployment` vs. `redeployment`:

| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
|---|---|---|

**Instance 2 — COORD — S2 — `new or existing applications and infrastructure components, and ICT services`**

Three categories (OR) coordinated. Compound scope:

- **R2:** Compound scope: any combination tested.

| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Separate | None in OJ |
| R2 | Compound | In-Directive: literal `or` |

R2 is the literal reading. S2.

*Berry anchor:* §5.4.7 (multi-way OR).

#### 6.1 Art. 25(1) — 12-modality open list (POLY/S3)

Art. 25(1) enumerates 12 specific test modalities under the `such as …` open-list signal. The structural pattern is **identical to CRA Annex I Part I §2(d)** (`authentication, identity or access management systems` under `including but not limited to`) and to the CRA Annex I enumeration generally.

- **Berry anchor:** §5.1 `such as` open-list marker; same pattern as CRA-C05 (Annex I Part I §2(d)).
- **Structural implication:** the 12 modalities are illustrative, not exhaustive. Financial entities may add (e.g. red team, social engineering, fuzz testing) but should not omit any of the 12 without justification.
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** CRA, NIS 2 (Art. 21(2)(e) `secure system development and maintenance` includes similar open enumerations), and DORA all converge on the open-list pattern. This is a **regulatory-drafting convention**, not an artefact of any single regulation.

#### 6.5 Art. 25(2) — Sectoral carve-out for CSD/CCP

Art. 25(2) imposes a **pre-deployment vulnerability assessment obligation on two specific entity types** (CSDs and CCPs). This is a sectoral carve-out unique in Ch. IV — no other clause names specific entity types.

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`central securities depositories`, `central counterparties`).
- **Structural implication:** CSDs and CCPs are **financial market infrastructures (FMIs)** with systemic importance. The pre-deployment assessment obligation reflects the FMI-specific risk profile (a deployment-induced vulnerability could cascade to the entire clearing or settlement system).
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** CRA does not impose pre-deployment obligations. NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(f) requires `policies and procedures for cryptography` but not pre-deployment assessments. **DORA Art. 25(2) is a sectoral specificity.**

#### 2.5 AIA-C05 — Art. 10(1) Data governance scope

**Source locus:** `High-risk AI systems which make use of techniques involving the training of AI models with data shall be developed on the basis of training, validation and testing data sets that meet the quality criteria referred to in paragraphs 2 to 5 whenever such data sets are used`.

* **VAG — S2** — `quality criteria` (deferred to paragraphs 2–5, themselves VAG-loaded).
* **POLY — S2** — `techniques involving the training of AI models` — does it include fine-tuning? Transfer learning? Reinforcement learning from human feedback? The clause does not enumerate.
* **COORD — S2** — `training, validation AND testing data sets` — 3-way AND of dataset categories.

**Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`quality`), §3.3.1 (`training`), §5.4.7.

#### 2.6 AIA-C06 — Art. 10(2) Data governance practices

**Source locus:** 8-element list (a)–(h) of data governance practices, including `(f) examination in view of possible biases … (g) appropriate measures to detect, prevent and mitigate possible biases`.

* **VAG — S2** — `appropriate`.
* **POLY — S3** — `biases` — statistical bias (sampling error) vs. algorithmic bias (unequal performance) vs. societal bias (discriminatory outcome). The clause does not differentiate; the three senses drive distinct detection methods.
* **COORD — S3** — 8-element closed list (a)–(h) AND-coordinated; within (g), `detect, prevent AND mitigate` is a 3-way AND of distinct activities. The cumulative obligation requires all 8 practices and all 3 bias activities.

**Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`appropriate`), §3.3.1 (`biases`), §5.4.7 (8-element list + nested 3-way AND).

#### 2.7 AIA-C07 — Art. 10(3) Dataset quality criteria

**Source locus:** `Training, validation and testing data sets shall be relevant, sufficiently representative, and to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete in view of the intended purpose`.

* **VAG — S3** — `relevant`, `sufficiently representative`, `to the best extent possible`, `free of errors`, `complete`. Five vague determiners in one sentence.
* **POLY — S2** — `representative` — demographically representative vs. statistically representative vs. contextually representative.
* **COORD — S3** — `relevant, sufficiently representative, AND to the best extent possible, free of errors AND complete`. The third coordinator (`and to the best extent possible`) introduces a soft hedge on what would otherwise be hard requirements (`free of errors`, `complete`). Berry §5.4.7: the parse tree admits distinct readings (are all five criteria hedged, or only the last two?).

**Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`relevant`, `sufficiently`, `free of errors`, `complete`), §5.4.7 (hedge-AND interaction).

#### 2.8 AIA-C08 — Art. 10(5) Special category data processing

**Source locus:** `To the extent that it is strictly necessary for the purpose of ensuring bias detection and correction … the providers of such systems may exceptionally process special categories of personal data, subject to appropriate safeguards … In addition … all the following conditions must be met: (a) … (b) … (c) … (d) … (e) … (f)`.

* **VAG — S3** — `strictly necessary`, `appropriate safeguards`, `state-of-the-art security and privacy-preserving measures`.
* **POLY — S2** — `state-of-the-art` — Berry §5.1 classic vague term (cf. GDPR C09, CRA C07). Time-dependent, jurisdiction-dependent.
* **COORD — S3** — Six cumulative conditions (a)–(f) all required (closed AND-list); the exception to GDPR Art. 9 prohibition is narrow in theory but each condition is qualitatively specified, leaving wide practical discretion.

**Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`strictly necessary`, `state-of-the-art`, `appropriate`), §5.4.7 (6-element AND-list).
