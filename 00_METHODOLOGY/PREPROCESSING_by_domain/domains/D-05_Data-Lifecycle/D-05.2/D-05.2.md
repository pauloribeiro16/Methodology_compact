**Participants (from CRDA):** GDPR, CRA, AI_Act

**Sole-authority note:** NIS2 and DORA are out-of-scope for D-05.2 per AEGIS taxonomy §4.1 — DORA Art. 12(1) backup policies specify scope and frequency but do not impose retention / archiving obligations as such. GDPR is the dedicated authority for retention; CRA addresses the distinct update-availability tail; AI Act addresses the distinct AI-log minimum floor.

##### Pair: GDPR ↔ CRA

**GDPR article (verbatim, Art. 5(1)(e) + Art. 30(1)(f)):**
> "Personal data shall be kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the personal data are processed ('storage limitation'). Personal data may be stored for longer periods insofar as the personal data will be processed solely for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes or statistical purposes in accordance with Article 89(1) subject to implementation of the appropriate technical and organisational measures required by this Regulation in order to safeguard the rights and freedoms of the data subject." — Art. 5(1)(e) GDPR
>
> "Each controller … shall maintain a record of processing activities under its responsibility … that contains … where possible, the envisaged time limits for erasure of the different categories of data." — Art. 30(1)(f) GDPR

**CRA article (verbatim, Annex I Part II (7)/(8) + Art. 13(8)/(9)):**
> "Securely distribute updates … to the users of products with digital elements, in a timely manner, including, where appropriate, through automatic updates." — CRA Annex I Part II (7)
>
> "Ensure that updates are disseminated free of charge, are clearly described, contain information on the security improvements and on the actions the user should take." — CRA Annex I Part II (8)
>
> "Where, after placing a product with digital elements on the market, the manufacturer becomes aware of a vulnerability affecting the security of the product, the manufacturer shall, without delay, disclose the vulnerability and any associated mitigations to the person or entity maintaining the product, where applicable, and to the market surveillance authorities, as well as, where the manufacturer becomes aware of an actively exploited vulnerability, to the users of the product." — CRA Art. 13(9) (vulnerability disclosure — adjacent but distinct from update availability)

(Note: the CRDA reasoning invokes a "10-year update-availability tail" derived from CRA Art. 13(18) + Annex I Part II (7)/(8) supporting-period accessibility, while the OJ literal anchor is the support-period floor of Art. 13(8) (5-year minimum) + the 10-year accessibility for Annex II information under Art. 13(18). The substantive distinction is that CRA's obligation is on *update artefacts* (firmware, patches, advisories) made available by the manufacturer — not on personal-data retention.)

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: personal data held by the controller.
- CRA scope: security-update artefacts (firmware, patches, advisories) made available by the manufacturer.
- Scope overlap: **N (typically)** — different data classes (personal data vs update artefacts).

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: continued storage of personal data beyond processing-purpose end.
- CRA trigger: ongoing availability of security updates across support period.
- Trigger alignment: **DIFFERENT** — retention vs availability.

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: no quantitative upper bound; "no longer than is necessary" is qualitative.
- CRA: support period is at least 5 years (Art. 13(8) CRA — the OJ-literal anchor); Annex II information must remain accessible for at least 10 years after placing on the market (Art. 13(18)). CRDA's "10-year tail" reading collapses these two provisions; the operative substance is "support period + 10-year information accessibility".
- Tension: **NONE — the scopes are disjoint.** The CRDA reasoning itself notes: "CRA's 10-year tail is about *update availability*, not personal-data retention … if a CRA-compliant product retains update-related logs containing personal data for 10 years, the GDPR retention ceiling may be violated."

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: data subject + supervisory authority (DPA).
- CRA: market surveillance authority (MSA) + users.

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: not codified; documented in RoPA (Art. 30(1)(f) "envisaged time limits for erasure").
- CRA: not codified; documented in Annex VII §3 risk-assessment record + Annex II information deliverable.

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** CONTRADICTORY (direction)
**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** **CORRECTED — SCOPE-DISJOINT**
**Reasoning:** The CRDA reasoning explicitly concedes: "These are not on the same axis (CRA's 10-year tail is about *update availability*, not personal-data retention)." With that concession, the "contradictory" label is internally inconsistent. Two regulations imposing limits on different data classes at different actors are **scope-disjoint**, not contradictory. A true contradiction requires mutually exclusive obligations on the same data at the same actor. There is no such overlap here: GDPR Art. 5(1)(e) caps the retention of *personal data*; CRA Annex I Part II (7)/(8) requires *update artefact* availability. If a CRA product's update-distribution logs contain personal data, the controller must decouple the log retention from the update availability via separation architecture — but that is a layering rule, not a contradiction.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? **Conditional (Y when a CRA update-distribution log contains personal data, the controller must apply GDPR Art. 5(1)(e) to the personal data portion; CRA Annex I Part II (7)/(8) applies to the artefact portion independently).** In the typical case (pure update artefacts with no personal data), there is no overlap. The pair is therefore scope-disjoint in baseline.

**Downstream implication (Phase 1C Doc 07):** Phase 2 obligation derivation must correct CRDA's "contradictory" label to "scope-disjoint" with separation-architecture requirement. The 3-axis retention policy (GDPR ceiling + CRA availability + AI Act floor) is correct as a *layering* rule, but the CRDA label "contradictory" is scope-incorrect. Phase 3 case generation must validate that CRA-regulated products with personal-data-containing update logs apply separation architecture (pseudonymisation, separate retention rules) to honour both regimes.

**Objective.** Data — across the data classes in scope — is retained for the period required to satisfy its declared purpose, with documented retention rationale and, where the underlying purpose changes, a reset of the retention clock. Long-term archiving or research-only retention is paired with safeguards proportional to the residual identifiability.

**Considerations.**
- Three participating regulations (GDPR, CRA, AI Act) each impose a retention/archiving duty on a distinct data class with a distinct obligation direction: GDPR Art. 5(1)(e) "no longer than is necessary" qualitative ceiling on personal data (controller-side, Art. 30(1)(f) RoPA time-limits + Art. 89(1) safeguards for the public-interest/research/statistical carve-out); CRA Art. 13(8) 5-year support period + Art. 13(9) 10-year update-availability tail + Art. 13(18) 10-year Annex II information accessibility on update artefacts (manufacturer-side, floor); AI Act Art. 19(1) ≥6-month floor on high-risk AI system logs (provider-side, "appropriate to the intended purpose" upward adjustment + GDPR escape clause).
- CRDA-deep verified relationships: GDPR↔CRA = CORRECTED from CONTRADICTORY (direction) to SCOPE-DISJOINT (the CRDA reasoning itself concedes CRA's 10-year tail is on update availability, not personal-data retention; the pair is scope-disjoint in baseline with separation architecture as the layering rule when the CRA update log contains personal data); GDPR↔AI_Act = CORRECTED from CONTRADICTORY (direction) to COMPLEMENTARY (floor-within-ceiling — the AI Act ≥6-month floor is a subset of GDPR's "as long as necessary" envelope, with operative range [6 months, "necessary"]); CRA↔AI_Act = SAME — DIFFERENT-PERSPECTIVE (update artefact availability vs AI log retention, different objects, actors, purposes).
- The 1 conditional emergent tension (3-way retention/obligation interaction on a single data flow when CRA update logs or AI logs contain personal data) is regulated by two layering rules: separation architecture (pseudonymisation, separate retention rules, separate storage paths) for the CRA↔GDPR overlap; floor-within-ceiling for the AI Act↔GDPR overlap.
- Implementation guidance: the three data classes are layered not collapsed — personal data is collected from data subjects (GDPR), update artefacts are produced by the manufacturer (CRA), AI logs are generated by the AI system (AI Act). A unified retention platform may serve the technical substrate but policy and evidence layers must remain class-segregated.

#### Scope-overlap principle (operational). The high-level SO
(`SO-D-05.2.HL`) engages on a given party **if and only if ≥2 applicable
regulations' data-class scope-determiners overlap on the same party**
(e.g. a GDPR controller that also manufactures CRA-regulated products
and operates a high-risk AI system processing personal data in both
the CRA update log and the AI log — a vertically-integrated
manufacturer-controller-provider on a 3-axis retention/archiving
architecture with the GDPR ceiling, the CRA availability floor, and
the AI Act log-retention floor all simultaneously engaged). Pure
co-applicability of disjoint-data-class regulations on different data
objects does **not** trigger cross-regulation aggregation — see the
Activation logic in the file header. For a 5-FTE SaaS startup with
GDPR + CRA applicability but no AI Act applicability, only the GDPR
and CRA sub-SOs activate; the high-level SO engages on the same
party if both apply, with the boundary between GDPR personal-data
retention ceiling and CRA update-artefact availability floor
documented as the controlled hand-off (separation architecture when
the CRA update log contains personal data; pure parallel obligations
when the log is purely technical). **The three data classes are
layered, not collapsed**: personal data is collected from data
subjects (GDPR); update artefacts are produced by the manufacturer
(CRA); AI logs are generated by the AI system (AI Act). The
high-level SO surfaces the common retention/archiving duty as the
3-axis layering rule but does **NOT** aggregate the per-reg data
class, obligation direction, trigger, threshold anchor, lifecycle
stage, recipient, or evidence template.

  surface the separation-architecture requirement explicitly when

  from CRDA-deep verified classifications: the high-level SO does NOT
  collapse the three data classes into a single retention policy.
  Personal data, update artefacts, and AI logs are **distinct data
  objects** at **distinct lifecycle stages** (controller-side
  processing vs manufacturer-side product support vs provider-side
  AI system log generation), with distinct obligation directions
  (ceiling vs floor vs floor), distinct threshold anchors (GDPR VAG
  vs CRA hard 10-year tail vs AI Act hard 6-month floor), and
  distinct evidence templates (Art. 30(1)(f) RoPA time-limits +
  Art. 89(1) safeguards vs Annex VII §3 risk-assessment + Annex II
  information deliverable + Annex VII §4 support-period determination
  vs Annex IV technical documentation + Art. 19(1) log-retention
  records). A unified data-retention platform may serve the
  technical substrate (e.g. a unified retention policy engine with
  per-data-class rules), but the policy and evidence layers must
  remain **class-segregated** for compliance demonstration. Phase
  1A must therefore emit **three checks before applying the
  high-level SO** (one per participating regulation) to confirm that
  the per-reg data class, obligation direction, trigger, threshold
  anchor, lifecycle stage, recipient, and evidence template are
  documented separately; the high-level SO surfaces the common
  retention/archiving duty as the 3-axis layering rule but does NOT
  aggregate the per-reg data classes.
- **Scope caveat (CRITICAL — CRA 10-year tail is on update
  artefacts, NOT on personal data)** — derived from CRDA-deep
  verified classifications: CRA Annex I Part II (7)/(8) and
  Art. 13(9)/(18) are about update artefact availability and Annex II
  information accessibility, NOT about personal-data retention. If
  a CRA-compliant product's update-distribution logs contain
  personal data, the controller must **decouple** the log retention
  from the update availability via separation architecture. The CRA
  10-year tail does NOT extend to personal data; the GDPR Art. 5(1)(e)
  `necessary` boundary remains the operative ceiling on the
  personal-data portion. The "10-year tail" of CRA is on update
  artefacts (firmware, patches, advisories — non-personal data) and
  Annex II information (product documentation, support-period end-
  date, intended purpose — non-personal data). For a CRA product
  with personal-data-containing update logs, the separation
  architecture is the controlled hand-off: pseudonymise the
  personal-data portion of the log, retain the pseudonymised log
  for the GDPR `necessary` boundary, and retain the update artefact
  for the CRA 10-year tail.
- **Scope caveat (CRITICAL — AI Act 6-month floor is a floor, not
  a ceiling)** — derived from CRDA-deep verified classifications: the
  AI Act Art. 19(1) `at least six months` is a **floor**, not a
  ceiling — the upward adjustment clause `appropriate to the
  intended purpose of the high-risk AI system` permits the provider
  to retain logs longer than 6 months when the intended purpose
  requires. The GDPR escape clause `unless provided otherwise in the
  applicable Union or national law, in particular Union law on the
  protection of personal data` defers to GDPR Art. 5(1)(e) when the
  log contains personal data. The operative retention range is
  [6 months, "necessary"] — the AI Act sets the floor, the GDPR sets
  the cap, the provider chooses the actual retention within the
  range based on the intended purpose. The high-level SO does NOT
  default to the 6-month floor; the default is the intended-purpose-
  calibrated retention within the floor-within-ceiling range.
- **Scope caveat (CRITICAL — CRA Art. 13(9) 10-year tail
  interpretation)** — derived from CRDA-deep verified classifications:
  the CRA Art. 13(9) `at least 10 years or for the remainder of the
  support period, whichever is longer` is a **twin-anchor** pattern
  where the 10-year floor and the support-period remainder are
  compared, and the longer of the two applies in each case. The
  chosen reading is R2 — the calculation is performed case-by-case on
  a per-update basis. A long-support product may have many updates
  subject to the support-period remainder rather than the fixed
  10-year floor. R1 (always 10 years) is rejected because it ignores
  the "whichever is longer" qualifier; R3 (always the support-period
  remainder) is similarly rejected because it ignores the 10-year
  floor when the support period is shorter than 10 years. The
  Art. 13(18) `accessible for at least 10 years` is similarly
  structured: the conjunction `support period + at least 10 years`
  is preserved, with the longer of the two governing. R2 is the
  per-update / per-document basis; R1 (10 years full stop) is
  rejected as narrower than the literal.

##### Emergent tensions (3+ regulations)
<!-- emergent: GDPR,CRA,AI_Act -->

- **Three-direction retention/obligation interaction.** GDPR says *upper limit*; AI Act says *lower floor for AI logs*; CRA says *lower floor for update artefacts*. The pairwise readings (GDPR↔CRA, GDPR↔AI_Act, CRA↔AI_Act) each handle two of the three directions, but the *non-pairwise observation* is that a single data stream (e.g., update logs from a CRA-classified high-risk AI) may need to satisfy all three simultaneously. Phase 2 must produce a retention-policy template that: (1) sets the AI Act 6-month floor as a hard minimum for AI logs; (2) caps retention at the GDPR "necessary" boundary; (3) ensures CRA update artefact availability is decoupled from personal-data retention via separation architecture.

##### Downstream implication
Phase 2 obligation derivation must produce a 3-axis retention policy: (1) GDPR ceiling (upper limit, Art. 5(1)(e)), (2) AI Act floor (≥6 months for high-risk AI logs), (3) CRA availability tail (10 years for security updates). Phase 3 case generation must validate the separation architecture in case companies.

##### Pair: GDPR ↔ CRA

**GDPR article (verbatim, Art. 5(1)(e) + Art. 30(1)(f)):**
> "Personal data shall be kept in a form which permits identification of data subjects for no longer than is necessary for the purposes for which the personal data are processed ('storage limitation'). Personal data may be stored for longer periods insofar as the personal data will be processed solely for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes or statistical purposes in accordance with Article 89(1) subject to implementation of the appropriate technical and organisational measures required by this Regulation in order to safeguard the rights and freedoms of the data subject." — Art. 5(1)(e) GDPR
>
> "Each controller … shall maintain a record of processing activities under its responsibility … that contains … where possible, the envisaged time limits for erasure of the different categories of data." — Art. 30(1)(f) GDPR

**CRA article (verbatim, Annex I Part II (7)/(8) + Art. 13(8)/(9)):**
> "Securely distribute updates … to the users of products with digital elements, in a timely manner, including, where appropriate, through automatic updates." — CRA Annex I Part II (7)
>
> "Ensure that updates are disseminated free of charge, are clearly described, contain information on the security improvements and on the actions the user should take." — CRA Annex I Part II (8)
>
> "Where, after placing a product with digital elements on the market, the manufacturer becomes aware of a vulnerability affecting the security of the product, the manufacturer shall, without delay, disclose the vulnerability and any associated mitigations to the person or entity maintaining the product, where applicable, and to the market surveillance authorities, as well as, where the manufacturer becomes aware of an actively exploited vulnerability, to the users of the product." — CRA Art. 13(9) (vulnerability disclosure — adjacent but distinct from update availability)

(Note: the CRDA reasoning invokes a "10-year update-availability tail" derived from CRA Art. 13(18) + Annex I Part II (7)/(8) supporting-period accessibility, while the OJ literal anchor is the support-period floor of Art. 13(8) (5-year minimum) + the 10-year accessibility for Annex II information under Art. 13(18). The substantive distinction is that CRA's obligation is on *update artefacts* (firmware, patches, advisories) made available by the manufacturer — not on personal-data retention.)

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: personal data held by the controller.
- CRA scope: security-update artefacts (firmware, patches, advisories) made available by the manufacturer.
- Scope overlap: **N (typically)** — different data classes (personal data vs update artefacts).

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: continued storage of personal data beyond processing-purpose end.
- CRA trigger: ongoing availability of security updates across support period.
- Trigger alignment: **DIFFERENT** — retention vs availability.

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: no quantitative upper bound; "no longer than is necessary" is qualitative.
- CRA: support period is at least 5 years (Art. 13(8) CRA — the OJ-literal anchor); Annex II information must remain accessible for at least 10 years after placing on the market (Art. 13(18)). CRDA's "10-year tail" reading collapses these two provisions; the operative substance is "support period + 10-year information accessibility".
- Tension: **NONE — the scopes are disjoint.** The CRDA reasoning itself notes: "CRA's 10-year tail is about *update availability*, not personal-data retention … if a CRA-compliant product retains update-related logs containing personal data for 10 years, the GDPR retention ceiling may be violated."

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: data subject + supervisory authority (DPA).
- CRA: market surveillance authority (MSA) + users.

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: not codified; documented in RoPA (Art. 30(1)(f) "envisaged time limits for erasure").
- CRA: not codified; documented in Annex VII §3 risk-assessment record + Annex II information deliverable.

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** CONTRADICTORY (direction)
**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** **CORRECTED — SCOPE-DISJOINT**
**Reasoning:** The CRDA reasoning explicitly concedes: "These are not on the same axis (CRA's 10-year tail is about *update availability*, not personal-data retention)." With that concession, the "contradictory" label is internally inconsistent. Two regulations imposing limits on different data classes at different actors are **scope-disjoint**, not contradictory. A true contradiction requires mutually exclusive obligations on the same data at the same actor. There is no such overlap here: GDPR Art. 5(1)(e) caps the retention of *personal data*; CRA Annex I Part II (7)/(8) requires *update artefact* availability. If a CRA product's update-distribution logs contain personal data, the controller must decouple the log retention from the update availability via separation architecture — but that is a layering rule, not a contradiction.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? **Conditional (Y when a CRA update-distribution log contains personal data, the controller must apply GDPR Art. 5(1)(e) to the personal data portion; CRA Annex I Part II (7)/(8) applies to the artefact portion independently).** In the typical case (pure update artefacts with no personal data), there is no overlap. The pair is therefore scope-disjoint in baseline.

**Downstream implication (Phase 1C Doc 07):** Phase 2 obligation derivation must correct CRDA's "contradictory" label to "scope-disjoint" with separation-architecture requirement. The 3-axis retention policy (GDPR ceiling + CRA availability + AI Act floor) is correct as a *layering* rule, but the CRDA label "contradictory" is scope-incorrect. Phase 3 case generation must validate that CRA-regulated products with personal-data-containing update logs apply separation architecture (pseudonymisation, separate retention rules) to honour both regimes.

**GDPR article (verbatim, Art. 5(1)(e)):**

**Trigger comparison:**

- GDPR: "no longer than is necessary" — qualitative upper bound.
- AI Act: ≥6-month minimum floor.
- Tension: **NONE — they are reconcilable.** The AI Act 6-month floor is a subset of GDPR's "as long as necessary" envelope. If the intended purpose requires 6+ months of logs, GDPR's "necessary" is satisfied for that period. The CRDA reasoning itself states: "The two are reconcilable: the AI Act 6-month floor is a subset of GDPR's 'as long as necessary'."

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: data subject + DPA.
- AI Act: market surveillance authority (MSA) + national competent authority.

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: not codified; documented in RoPA (Art. 30(1)(f)).
- AI Act: not codified for content; Art. 19(1) sets the floor.

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** CONTRADICTORY (direction)
**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** **CORRECTED — COMPLEMENTARY** (floor-within-ceiling)
**Reasoning:** The CRDA reasoning explicitly states: "The two are reconcilable: the AI Act 6-month floor is a subset of GDPR's 'as long as necessary'." A floor-within-ceiling relationship is the textbook **complementary** pattern — one regulation sets a hard floor (≥6 months), the other sets a qualitative ceiling (no longer than necessary), and the operative range is the intersection [6 months, "necessary"]. There is no contradiction. The CRDA "contradictory" label is scope-incorrect. Two regulators that set the same boundary at different sides of an interval do not contradict each other; they jointly bound the admissible range.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? **Y (when AI-system logs contain personal data, both GDPR and AI Act apply on the same log set at the same controller / provider)**. The two obligations are reconcilable within the intersection [6 months, "necessary"]. The pair is substantive-complementary, not scope-disjoint.

**Downstream implication (Phase 1C Doc 07):** Phase 2 obligation derivation must codify AI Act Art. 19(1) as a *floor* within the GDPR Art. 5(1)(e) *ceiling* envelope. The CRDA "contradictory" label should be corrected to "complementary". Phase 3 case generation must validate that high-risk AI providers set the AI Act 6-month floor as a hard minimum and the GDPR "necessary" boundary as the cap.

#### 6.5 Art. 21 — single EU Hub feasibility report (RTS-style structural delegation)

Art. 21 mandates a **feasibility report** by the ESAs Joint Committee (in consultation with ECB and ENISA) on the potential centralisation of incident reporting through a **single EU Hub**. The report must comprise **7 elements** (a)–(g).

This is **not an obligation on financial entities** — it is a meta-procedural mandate on the ESAs. But its outcome (whether or not a single EU Hub is established) could materially alter the entity-side reporting architecture. As of Q1 2025 (post the 17 January 2025 deadline), the ESAs Joint Committee report was published; the legislative follow-up is uncertain.

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`single EU Hub`); §5.1 (`feasibility`); §3.3.1 (`interoperability`).
- **Structural implication:** the **single EU Hub** is structurally analogous to the **NIS 2 single point of contact (SPOC)** (NIS 2 Art. 12) and the **CER Directive critical-entity framework** but at a higher integration level.

#### 2.6 AIA-C06 — Art. 10(2) Data governance practices

**Source locus:** 8-element list (a)–(h) of data governance practices, including `(f) examination in view of possible biases … (g) appropriate measures to detect, prevent and mitigate possible biases`.

* **VAG — S2** — `appropriate`.
* **POLY — S3** — `biases` — statistical bias (sampling error) vs. algorithmic bias (unequal performance) vs. societal bias (discriminatory outcome). The clause does not differentiate; the three senses drive distinct detection methods.
* **COORD — S3** — 8-element closed list (a)–(h) AND-coordinated; within (g), `detect, prevent AND mitigate` is a 3-way AND of distinct activities. The cumulative obligation requires all 8 practices and all 3 bias activities.

**Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`appropriate`), §3.3.1 (`biases`), §5.4.7 (8-element list + nested 3-way AND).

#### 2.7 AIA-C07 — Art. 10(3) Dataset quality criteria

**Source locus:** `Training, validation and testing data sets shall be relevant, sufficiently representative, and to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete in view of the intended purpose`.

* **VAG — S3** — `relevant`, `sufficiently representative`, `to the best extent possible`, `free of errors`, `complete`. Five vague determiners in one sentence.
* **POLY — S2** — `representative` — demographically representative vs. statistically representative vs. contextually representative.
* **COORD — S3** — `relevant, sufficiently representative, AND to the best extent possible, free of errors AND complete`. The third coordinator (`and to the best extent possible`) introduces a soft hedge on what would otherwise be hard requirements (`free of errors`, `complete`). **Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`relevant`, `sufficiently`, `free of errors`, `complete`), §5.4.7 (hedge-AND interaction).
