**Objective.** Security-relevant events affecting the entity's systems, products, services, components and data are detected and triaged with documented trigger moments that anchor the downstream notification and response pipelines.

**Considerations.**
- All four participating regulations (GDPR, NIS 2, CRA, DORA) impose a detection-and-triage duty but each targets a distinct detection layer and anchors to a distinct threshold: GDPR's data-flow-boundary awareness of personal-data breach (Art. 4(12) + Art. 33(1) `becoming aware` — the awareness anchor is GDPR-unique and drives the 72h clock); NIS 2's entity-side all-hazards monitoring with the 6-action incident-handling lifecycle covering `detect` (Art. 21(2) chapeau + Recital 56 + Art. 6(8) + Art. 21(2)(b)); CRA's product-side intra-product state-change visibility (Annex I Part I (2)(l); the recording-and-monitoring obligation is CRA-unique and provides the manufacturer's view inside the product boundary); DORA's financial-entity-side multi-class anomalous-activity detection with SPOF identification and threshold-driven escalation (Art. 10(1) + Art. 10(2); the multi-layer + auto-alert + SPOF pattern is DORA-unique).
- The high-level SO surfaces the common detection-and-triage duty (detect, document the trigger moment, triage, route to the downstream response/notification pipeline); the per-regulation perspective (detection layer, trigger anchor, threshold qualifier, evidence template) is preserved in the four sub-SOs.
- A single organisation across all four regulators must operate a multi-layer detection architecture with explicit layer hand-offs: data-flow detection feeds entity detection at the controller/NSIE boundary; entity detection feeds product detection via deployment (CRA products instrument their intra-product visibility to feed the deployer-side detection); product detection feeds back into DORA infrastructure detection via integration. For parties that touch only one regulatory scope, only the triggered sub-SO applies.
- The high-level SO does NOT collapse the four detection layers into a single detection event — the layers detect different objects (personal-data breach vs all-hazards event vs intra-product state change vs anomalous ICT activity) and produce different trigger moments (GDPR `becoming aware`, NIS 2 detection-of-adverse-event, CRA product-internal-event-recording, DORA anomalous-activity-threshold).
- The high-level SO is honesty-preserving about scope-disjoint cases: for a 5-FTE SaaS startup with GDPR + CRA applicability but no NIS 2 / DORA applicability, only the GDPR and CRA sub-SOs activate; the high-level SO engages on the same party if both apply, with the boundary between data-flow detection (GDPR) and product-internal detection (CRA) documented as the controlled hand-off.
- DORA's multi-layer + SPOF-identification detection (Art. 10(1)/(2)) is the strictest detection-architecture floor and supports the NIS 2 `where appropriate` test on detection depth for financial-sector entities also within retained NIS 2 scope.

#### Scope-overlap principle (operational). The high-level SO
(`SO-D-04.1.HL`) engages on a given party **if and only if ≥2 applicable
regulations' detection-layer scope-determiners overlap on the same
party** (e.g. a controller that is also a NIS 2 essential entity
operating CRA-regulated products and running a DORA-class financial ICT
system — a vertically-integrated manufacturer-controller on a
financial-services stack with a multi-layer detection architecture).
Pure co-applicability of disjoint-layer regulations on different
detection objects does **not** trigger cross-regulation aggregation —
see the Activation logic in the file header. For a 5-FTE SaaS startup
with GDPR + CRA applicability but no NIS 2 / DORA applicability, only
the GDPR and CRA sub-SOs activate; the high-level SO engages on the
same party if both apply, with the boundary between data-flow detection
(GDPR) and product-internal detection (CRA) documented as the
controlled hand-off. **The four detection layers are layered, not
collapsed**: data-flow events are detected at the controller boundary
(GDPR); entity-level events are detected at the entity boundary
(NIS 2); product-internal events are detected inside the product (CRA);
anomalous-ICT events are detected at the financial-entity
infrastructure (DORA). The high-level SO surfaces the common
detection-and-triage duty but does **NOT** aggregate the per-reg

##### Emergent tensions (3+ regulations)
None. The pairwise readings above are additive — no 3-way contradiction or non-pairwise-surfacing observation. The 4-way detection layer (entity / product / personal-data / financial-entity) is layered, not contradictory.

##### SR cross-validation
NIST CSF 2.0 coverage is anchored on **DE.CM-01** (Networks monitored) and **DE.CM-09** (Computing hardware monitored) by GDPR, NIS2, and DORA; CRA anchors on **PR.PS-04** (Software logs recorded) and **DE.AE-08** (Vulnerabilities identified). The two anchor families are complementary: DE.CM-* covers the network/hardware perimeter (entity level) and PR.PS-04/DE.AE-08 covers the product-internal surface. No DE.CM or DE.AE conflict; mapping is consistent across regulations. RS.MA-* (Incident management executed) appears only in CRA (SR-CRA-027's secondary mapping) and DORA, reflecting the difference between detection-only and detection+response-triggering obligations.

##### Downstream implication
Phase 2 obligation derivation must distinguish detection-architecture obligations (DORA Art. 10, NIS2 Art. 21(2)(b)) from detection-trigger obligations (GDPR Art. 33(1) `becoming aware`). The methodology must ensure a financial entity implementing both DORA and GDPR produces a single integrated detection pipeline that satisfies both; the SO-GDPR-008 "becoming aware" anchor must be re-interpreted as the moment a DORA-detected event crosses the personal-data threshold.

#### D-04.1 Incident Detection & Triage

**Participants (from CRDA):** GDPR, NIS2, CRA, DORA

**Sole-authority note:** AI Act is out-of-scope for D-04.1 per AEGIS taxonomy §4.1; AI Act detection obligations are coupled to the Art. 73/55 reporting triggers (D-04.3) and not standalone.

##### Pair: GDPR ↔ NIS2

**GDPR article (verbatim):**
> Art. 32(1)(b) requires the ability to ensure ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability and resilience of processing systems and services. Art. 33(1) creates the 72-hour clock from the moment of becoming aware; awareness requires detection capability. Art. 4(12) defines a personal data breach as a breach of security leading to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to personal data transmitted, stored or otherwise processed.

**NIS2 article (verbatim):**
> Article 21(2)(b) of Directive (EU) 2022/2555 requires essential and important entities to take measures regarding incident handling. Article 6(8) defines `incident handling` as all actions and procedures aiming at, where appropriate, preventing, detecting, analysing, containing and responding to and recovering from an incident (OJ L 333, 27.12.2022, p. 113) — a six-action process anchored in the Article 21(2) all-hazards approach.

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: detection of personal-data breaches enabling the 72-hour clock.
- NIS2 scope: entity-level all-hazards incident-handling detection.
- Scope overlap: Conditional where the controller is also a NIS2 entity and the breach affects NIS-scoped systems.

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: awareness of a personal-data breach.
- NIS2 trigger: awareness of a significant incident under Art. 23(3) test A or test B.
- Trigger alignment: DIFFERENT.

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: 72-hour clock to the supervisory authority.
- NIS2: 24-hour early warning + 72-hour incident notification + 1-month final report.
- Tension: NONE.

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: supervisory authority.
- NIS2: CSIRT or competent authority.

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: nature, contact, consequences, measures per Art. 33(3).
- NIS2: severity, impact, indicators of compromise, cross-border, suspected unlawful or malicious acts.

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** COMPLEMENTARY

**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** SAME — COMPLEMENTARY

**Reasoning (1-2 paragraphs):** The OJ text supports complementarity. GDPR is breach-detection-anchored to the 72-hour clock; NIS2 is incident-handling-anchored to the six-action lifecycle. The same detection event can feed both pipelines, but the downstream notifications go to different recipients under different templates.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? Conditional
- If N or Conditional: the CRDA "contradictory" classification is **scope-incorrect**; it should be "scope-disjoint" in baseline. This pair was not classified contradictory.

**Downstream implication (1-2 sentences):** Doc 07 should design the detection pipeline to publish awareness events to both a GDPR-aware channel and a NIS-aware channel, with the supervisory and CSIRT routes in parallel.

# Part 4 — Relevant Ambiguity (by regulation)

## GDPR

##### Art. 10 — Processing of criminal convictions

**Clause: GDPR-CL25** | type: principle (criminal data) | obligatedParty: CONTROLLER (official authority only for comprehensive registers) | obligationType: PER-RECORD

**Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`appropriate safeguards`, `comprehensive register`).

**Instance 1 — VAG / S3 / `comprehensive register`**

`comprehensive register` is undefined; the term encompasses national criminal-record databases but the boundary with sectoral registers (e.g. fraud registers, sanctions lists, AML/KYC databases) is contested.

**Variant readings:**
| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | `Comprehensive register` = national criminal-records database (e.g. UK's Police National Computer). | Recital 50 (non-binding); Member State practice. |
| R2 | `Comprehensive register` = any database covering all convictions of a population segment (including fraud registers, sex-offender registers). | EDPB Guidelines on criminal-conviction data. |
| R3 | `Comprehensive register` = any database whose coverage exceeds sectoral purpose. | Loose reading. |

R1 vs R3 yield materially distinct scope of `official authority` control.

#### Verbatim (with highlighting)

> **Article 11**
>
> 1. The controller shall not be obliged to maintain, acquire or process **additional information** to identify the data subject for the sole purpose of complying with this Regulation in order to comply with the obligations laid down in Articles 5 to 9 (...).

Where in cases referred to in this Article the controller is able to demonstrate that it is not in a position to identify the data subject, the controller shall inform the data subject accordingly, if possible. ##### Art. 32(1) — Security measures (open list)

**Clause: GDPR-CP15** | type: security | obligatedParty: CONTROLLER + PROCESSOR | obligationType: CONTINUOUS

**Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`state of the art`, `appropriate`, `as appropriate`), §5.4.7 (4-element list), §3.3.1 (CIA+R polysemy).

**Instance 1 — VAG / S3 / `state of the art`**

Cross-clause propagation — same as Art. 25(1). S3 because security-engineering scope is material to compliance.

**Instance 2 — COORD / S3 / `(a) AND (b) AND (c) AND (d)`**

The 4-element list `(a) pseudonymisation/encryption, (b) CIA+R, (c) restore-in-timely-manner, (d) regular-testing` is closed (and). All 4 required.

**Instance 3 — POLY+VAG / S3 / `in a timely manner` (Art. 32(1)(c))**

`Timely manner` is the textbook Berry-flagged temporal vague phrase. Recital 49 (non-binding) attempts to fix: "restore the availability and access as soon as possible". The cross-reference is non-binding and the operational meaning drifts.

**Variant readings:**
| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | `Timely` = RTO defined per processing activity (objective measure). | Industry standard (BCP/DRP best practice). |
| R2 | `Timely` = as-soon-as-practicable for the specific incident. | EDPB Guidelines 7/2019 (generic). |

R1 vs R2 yield materially distinct BCP/DRP engineering scope.

### Article 33 — Breach notification to supervisory authority

#### Verbatim (with highlighting)

> **Article 33**
> **Notification of a personal data breach to the supervisory authority**
>
> 1. In the case of a personal data breach, the controller shall **without undue delay** and, **where feasible**, **not later than 72 hours after having become aware of it**, notify the personal data breach to the supervisory authority competent in accordance with Article 55, unless the personal data breach is **unlikely to result in a risk** to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. Where the notification to the supervisory authority is not made within 72 hours, it shall be accompanied by reasons for the delay.

> 2. The processor shall notify the controller **without undue delay** after becoming aware of a personal data breach.

> 3. The notification referred to in paragraph 1 shall at least:

> (a) describe the nature of the personal data breach including **where possible**, the categories and **approximate number** of data subjects concerned and the categories and approximate number of personal data records concerned;

> (b) communicate the name and contact details of the data protection officer or other contact point where more information can be obtained;

> (c) describe the **likely consequences** of the personal data breach;

> (d) describe the measures taken or proposed to be taken by the controller to address the personal data breach, including, where appropriate, measures to mitigate its possible adverse effects.

#### Clause breakdown

##### Art. 33(1) — 72-hour notification

**Clause: GDPR-CP17** | type: breach notification | obligatedParty: CONTROLLER | obligationType: TRIGGERED (per breach)

**Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`without undue delay`, `where feasible`, `unlikely`), §5.7 (temporal operators), §3.3.1 (`becoming aware` polysemy).

**Instance 1 — VAG / S3 / `without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours`**

Twin temporal operators (Berry §5.1, §5.7) — `without undue delay` is soft, `72 hours` is hard, `where feasible` softens the hard. Interaction undefined.

**Instance 2 — SCOPE-Q / S3 / `becoming aware of it`**

The awareness trigger is polysemous (cf. CRA C25, NIS 2 C25 — same pattern across regulations).

**Instance 3 — POLY+VAG / S3 / `unlikely to result in a risk`**

`Unlikely` is qualitative; `risk` is the recurring polysemy (multiple threshold definitions across GDPR).

**Variant readings:**
| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Notification required unless certainty-of-no-risk; precautionary principle. | EDPB Guidelines 9/2022 on breach notification. |
| R2 | Notification required only when risk threshold is met; controller assessment suffices. | Industry reading. |

R1 vs R2 yield materially distinct breach-response workflows.

**Source locus:**

> Products with digital elements shall be made available on the market only where:

| R3 | Baseline | None in OJ |

R2 is the literal reading (Part I (1) is mandatory; Part I (2) is conditional). S2.

*Berry anchor:* §3.3.1 (`essential`).

**Instance 2 — POLY — S2 — `meet`**

- **R2:** Self-declare conformity.

| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |

### 5. T3-vs-text gaps

This section catalogues **inconsistencies between the v0.1 T3 mapping** and the OJ text. Per pilot convention, T3-vs-text gaps are flagged separately from linguistic ambiguities of the regulation (they are inconsistencies of the mapping layer, not of the text).

#### 6.1 Art. 4 propagation pattern — three distinct proportionality regimes

Art. 4 establishes **three distinct proportionality regimes** that operate simultaneously:

- **Art. 4(1) — Ch. II proportionality** (CL4-1): default for the ICT risk management framework.
- **Art. 4(2) — Ch. III, IV, V Sec. I proportionality** (CL4-2): default for incident reporting, testing, and third-party risk.
- **Art. 4(3) — CA-review proportionality** (CL4-3): meta-provision requiring CAs to consider proportionality when reviewing the framework.

The three regimes are **not symmetric**: Art. 4(1) is self-implementing (the entity applies it directly); Art. 4(2) defers to chapter-specific clauses (Art. 16 simplification, Art. 25(3) microenterprise exception); Art. 4(3) is addressed to CAs.

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`principle of proportionality`); §5.1 (`proportionate`).
- **Structural implication:** the proportionality overlay is **inherited by every Ch. II–V Sec. I clause** in v0.2. Every clause card in `02b`–`05a` carries `proportionality-status: Art. 4 applies`. The v0.2 convention was established in the pilot file (`01_DORA_Art3_Definitions.md` §2 note on Art. 4).
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** Art. 4's proportionality pattern mirrors **NIS 2 Art. 1(2)** (proportionality in scope) and **CRA Art. 1(2)** (proportionality for microenterprises and SMEs). DORA Art. 4 is more elaborate than either, providing explicit CA-review (CL4-3) that NIS 2 and CRA do not have.

#### 6.3 Art. 6 chapeau — the framework's three-attribute triad

Art. 6(1) opens with `sound, comprehensive and well-documented` (three attributes, AND). This is the **framework-level analog** of:

- **Art. 24(1) `sound and comprehensive`** (testing programme, two attributes; CL24-1)
- **CRA Annex I Part I §1 chapeau** `products with digital elements shall be designed, developed and produced …` (manufacturing lifecycle, three verbs; CRA-CL14)
- **NIS 2 Art. 21(1) `appropriate and proportionate technical, operational and organisational measures`** (measures chapeau)

The 3-attribute triad (`sound, comprehensive and well-documented`) is **the most demanding framework chapeau in the 5-regulation corpus** — it requires three distinct qualities (effectiveness, completeness, traceability). CRA Annex I Part I §1 has three verbs (design, develop, produce) but they describe **processes** not qualities. DORA's triad describes **qualities of an artifact** (the framework itself), which is a different kind of obligation.

- **Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`sound`, `comprehensive`, `well-documented`); §5.4.7 (AND); §5.5 (parallelism).
- **Structural implication:** the framework's three attributes set the bar for every Ch. II–V Sec. I clause. Any clause that fails one of the three (e.g. a framework that is `comprehensive and well-documented` but not `sound`) would be non-compliant.
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** the triad is the most explicit framework-quality specification in the corpus. NIS 2 Art. 21(1) uses `appropriate and proportionate` (2 qualifiers); CRA Annex I Part I uses process verbs; GDPR Art. 24 uses `appropriate` (1 qualifier). DORA's 3-attribute triad is the most demanding.

#### 6.6 Art. 6(8) — the 8-point resilience strategy

Art. 6(8) enumerates **8 strategy elements** (open list: `include … by`). The 8 elements cover the strategy's full lifecycle: (a) business alignment, (b) risk tolerance, (c) KPIs/KRMs, (d) reference architecture, (e) detection/prevention, (f) current state evidence, (g) testing, (h) communication.

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`include … by`); §5.4.7 (8-way enumeration).
- **Structural implication:** the 8-point strategy is the **operational mirror** of the framework's three-attribute triad (CL6-1 Instance 1). Where the triad describes the framework's qualities, the 8 elements describe its **contents**. A framework that is `sound, comprehensive and well-documented` and contains all 8 elements satisfies the chapeau.
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** the open-list pattern is the **regulatory-drafting convention** shared with CRA Annex I Part I (multiple open enumerations) and NIS 2 Art. 21(2) (10 sub-points). DORA's 8 elements are **more strategic** (KPIs, risk tolerance, communication) than CRA's (technical requirements) and **more operational** than NIS 2's (governance policies).

#### 4.1 CL17-1 — Art. 17(1) — Process establishment

**Source locus:** Art. 17(1).

> Financial entities shall **define, establish and implement** an ICT-related incident management process to detect, manage and notify ICT-related incidents.

**Instance 1 — COORD — S2 — `define, establish and implement`**

Three coordinated verbs; the standard for each is undefined. Same pattern as DORA-D01 `build, assure and review` and Art. 24(1) `establish, maintain and review` (CL24-1 in Ch. IV file).

- **R1:** Three discrete lifecycle phases (define-then-establish-then-implement).
- **R2:** Three aspects of a single continuous process.
- **R3:** Three independent control families (define policy, establish system, implement operationally).

Three coordinated purposes of the process. Same Berry pattern.

#### 4.27 CL21-1 — Art. 21(1)+(2) — Single EU Hub feasibility mandate

**Source locus:** Art. 21(1) and 21(2).

> 1. The ESAs, through the Joint Committee, and in consultation with the ECB and ENISA, shall prepare a joint report assessing the **feasibility of further centralisation of incident reporting through the establishment of a single EU Hub for major ICT-related incident reporting** by financial entities. The joint report shall explore ways to facilitate the flow of ICT-related incident reporting, reduce associated costs and underpin thematic analyses with a view to enhancing supervisory convergence.
>
> 2. The joint report referred to in paragraph 1 shall comprise at least the following elements:
>
> (a) prerequisites for the establishment of a single EU Hub;
>
> (b) benefits, limitations and risks, including risks associated with the high concentration of sensitive information;
>
> (c) the necessary capability to ensure interoperability with regard to other relevant reporting schemes;
>
> (d) elements of operational management;
>
> (e) conditions of membership;
>
> (f) technical arrangements for financial entities and national competent authorities to access the single EU Hub;
>
> (g) a preliminary assessment of financial costs incurred by setting-up the operational platform supporting the single EU Hub, including the requisite expertise.

**Instance 1 — VAG — S3 — `single EU Hub`**

`single` + `Hub` — the architectural choice is undefined.

- **R1:** Single centralised data platform (one location, all CAs access).
- **R2:** Single API gateway (each CA holds data, common API).
- **R3:** Single standard (distributed but interoperable).
