#### D-01.1 Data at Rest Encryption

<!-- participants: GDPR, NIS2, CRA, DORA; AI_Act absent -->

**Participants (from CRDA):** GDPR, NIS2, CRA, DORA (AI_Act is not present; AI Act §D-01.1 marks CRA Annex I (2)(e) as sole authority per taxonomy §4.1).

##### Pair: GDPR ↔ NIS2

**GDPR article (verbatim from SR-GDPR-001, Art. 5(1)(f) + Art. 32(1)(a)/(b)):**
> Art. 5(1)(f) integrity and confidentiality … processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data … protection against unauthorised or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction or damage, using appropriate technical or organisational measures. Art. 32(1)(b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability and resilience of processing systems and services.

**NIS 2 article (verbatim from SR-NIS2-001, Art. 21(2)(h)):**
> Article 21(2)(h) of Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (NIS2) requires essential and important entities to establish policies and procedures regarding the use of cryptography and, where appropriate, encryption.

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: "personal data" (Art. 4(1) identifiability; pseudonymised data still in scope per CJEU Breyer and IAB Europe C-604/22).
- NIS 2 scope: "network and information systems" holding data that is "stored, transmitted or otherwise processed" (Art. 6(1)+(2)) — broader than personal data; includes operational, ICT-telemetry, business-confidential, and machine-generated data.
- Scope overlap: Y when the at-rest datastore holds personal data; Y (conditional) — typically the same storage holds both personal and non-personal data in modern stacks.

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: continuous obligation under Art. 5(1)(f) + Art. 32(1) (no event trigger; risk-anchored floor).
- NIS 2 trigger: continuous obligation under Art. 21(2)(h) + Art. 21(1) appropriate-and-proportionate.
- Trigger alignment: SAME (both continuous, both risk-anchored; no event-trigger divergence).

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: "appropriate" anchored to the five Art. 32(1) preamble factors (state of the art, costs, nature, scope, context, purposes, risks).
- NIS 2: "where appropriate" anchored to the same Art. 21(1) preamble plus the "appropriate and proportionate" qualifier (state of the art, standards, cost, exposure, size, likelihood, severity).
- Tension: NONE — the two thresholds converge on risk-anchored proportionality; the NIS 2 "where appropriate" hedge admits a narrower scope than GDPR's "appropriate", but where NIS 2 is narrower, GDPR is stricter (always applicable to personal data).

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: supervisory authority (Art. 58 enforcement); data subjects are notified on breach if Art. 34(1) high-risk threshold met (or carve-out under Art. 34(3)(a)).
- NIS 2: CSIRT or competent authority (Art. 23 reporting cascade); no direct data-subject notification.

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: technical-and-organis measures + accountability demonstrability under Art. 5(2).
- NIS 2: documented cryptography policy (SR-NIS2-001 title), with risk-anchored policy-and-procedure evidence.

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** COMPLEMENTARY.

**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** SAME (CORRECTED — terminology only, not substance).

**Reasoning (1-2 paragraphs):**
The OJ texts impose the same substantive obligation (encryption of data at rest where the risk assessment indicates) anchored to the same proportionality test (state of the art, risk factors). The two differ in (a) scope qualifier ("personal data" vs "network and information systems") — which makes NIS 2 broader; (b) anchor qualifier ("appropriate" vs "where appropriate") — which makes GDPR stricter for personal data; and (c) the GDPR-only existence of the Art. 34(3)(a) unintelligibility carve-out from data-subject notification. These are scope-depth and wording differences on a common obligation, not fundamentally different regulatory framings. CRDA's COMPLEMENTARY is consistent with this reading but understates the convergence: the two are best described as EQUIVALENT obligations on the same event (encryption at rest of personal data on a NIS 2 essential entity that is also a GDPR controller). The compliance population is the same party on the same artefact.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Y — the GDPR controller that is also a NIS 2 essential entity bears both obligations on the same datastore.

- If Y: the COMPLEMENTARY classification is correct; the practical reconciliation is stacked documentation (the GDPR Article 32 risk assessment doubles as the NIS 2 cryptography-policy risk rationale).

**Downstream implication (1-2 sentences):** For Phase 1C Doc 07, D-01.1 is a **stack-merge with documentation** sub-domain: a single at-rest encryption deployment (e.g. approved symmetric cipher with appropriate key custody under separate logical access) plus the joint Article 32 / Art. 21(2)(h) risk-assessment artefact discharges both obligations on a NIS 2 essential entity that is a GDPR controller.

##### Pair: GDPR ↔ CRA

**GDPR article (verbatim from SR-GDPR-001, Art. 5(1)(f) + Art. 32(1)(a)):**
> Art. 32(1)(a) pseudonymisation and encryption of personal data … as appropriate technical measures.

**CRA article (verbatim from SR-CRA-001 + SR-CRA-002, Annex I Part I (1) + (2)(e)):**
> Annex I Part I (1) ensure an appropriate level of cybersecurity based on the risks presented by the product. Annex I Part I (2)(e) the confidentiality of stored, transmitted or otherwise processed data — personal or other — by encrypting relevant data at rest or in transit using state-of-the-art mechanisms, "or by using other technical means."

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: personal data.
- CRA scope: "personal or other" data stored by a product with digital elements (Art. 3(47) cross-references GDPR Art. 4(1) personal-data definition; Annex I extends to non-personal data).
- Scope overlap: Y when the CRA-regulated product processes personal data; Y (conditional).

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: continuous risk-anchored.
- CRA trigger: continuous product-side baseline (Art. 13 umbrella + Annex I chapeau).
- Trigger alignment: SAME (continuous on both legs).

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: "appropriate" (risk-based).
- CRA: "state-of-the-art mechanisms" (R2 — harmonised-standards floor per SR-CRA-001 ambiguity_notes; bench-anchored to support-period commencement date).
- Tension: NONE — the CRA state-of-the-art baseline is **stricter** than GDPR's risk-based "appropriate" once harmonised standards are present; in their absence, the SR-CRA-001 reading imports ISO/NIST-published standards at support-period commencement.

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: supervisory authority.
- CRA: market surveillance authority + ENISA (Ancillary — not a recipient per se).

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: risk assessment + accountability.
- CRA: technical documentation under Annex VII §2/§3 + conformity assessment (Module B or H depending on criticality).

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** COMPLEMENTARY.

**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** SAME (CORRECTED — wording only).

**Reasoning:** The two are the same substantive obligation (encryption at rest to protect personal data) with different anchors: GDPR Art. 32(1)(a) is risk-based; CRA Annex I (2)(e) is state-of-the-art + harmonised-standards. Where the two apply on the same artefact (a CRA-regulated product that processes personal data), the CRA baseline is stricter; meeting the CRA floor discharges the GDPR obligation on the at-rest dimension for that product. CRDA's COMPLEMENTARY is correct in framing but the practical effect on a single product is that CRA is the floor and GDPR is automatically satisfied; COMPLEMENTARY slightly understates that the two converge.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Conditional — disjoint in the baseline case (CRA manufacturer is not the GDPR controller; the SaaS deployer is the GDPR controller inheriting CRA's product-side baseline). Y when the same party is both manufacturer and controller (integrated vendors).

- **P0 note:** This is the pattern the Orchestrator should flag. For the SaaS-using-CRA-product case, the CRDA "C" classification is technically correct on the underlying concern but **does not produce a single compliance task** for either party. Phase 1C Doc 07 should treat this pair as **scope-disjoint in baseline; Y on integrated-stack**.

**Downstream implication:** For Phase 1C Doc 07, this is a **disjoint baseline / scope-conditional** pair: a non-integrated SaaS-uses-CRA-product company has separate compliance tracks (CRA SBOM intake from vendor + GDPR at-rest implementation by controller); an integrated manufacturer-controller has one combined documentation pack.

**DORA article (verbatim from SR-DORA-001, Art. 9(2)):**
> Art. 9(2) ICT security policies, procedures, protocols and tools that aim to ensure the resilience, continuity and availability of ICT systems and to maintain high standards of availability, authenticity, integrity and confidentiality of data, whether at rest, in use or in transit.

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: personal data.
- DORA scope: "data" of the financial entity (4×3 CIA+A matrix; the OJ does not say "personal data"; this is all ICT-supported data including non-personal).
- Scope overlap: Y (conditional) when the financial entity processes personal data — typically true.

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: continuous + on material change.
- DORA trigger: continuous + annual scenario review (Art. 7(2) sentence 2 "at least yearly" hard anchor; Art. 9(4) chapeau continuous).
- Trigger alignment: PARTIAL (DORA adds the annual scenario-review trigger; GDPR adds the Art. 35(11) DPIA-on-change trigger).

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: "appropriate" / risk-based.
- DORA: "high standards" (VAG-S3; R1 best practice / R2 published standards / R3 frontier research; reading chosen in SR-DORA-001: R1 with R2 dominant EU supervisory reading).
- Tension: NONE on the substantive threshold — both are risk-anchored; DORA's "high standards" anchor is qualitatively similar but OJ-text softer than CRA's "state-of-the-art". The temporal anchor divergence (GDPR "regularly" vs DORA "continuous" + "at least yearly") is in D-02.1, not D-01.1.

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: supervisory authority.
- DORA: competent authority (national CA, ESAs depending on entity type).

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: technical-and-organis measures + DPIA.
- DORA: ICT risk-management framework under Art. 6(8) + classification-driven cipher strength under Art. 9(4)(d) (relevant for D-01.3).

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** COMPLEMENTARY.

**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** SAME (CORRECTED — wording only).

**Reasoning:** The two obligations are the same on the at-rest dimension: high-standards/at-appropriate-level encryption of data at rest. The principal divergence (DORA 4×3 CIA+A vs GDPR 3×CIA) is in scope dimensions, not in the at-rest/encryption cell where they overlap. Both share the same OJ reality: a single at-rest control deployed on a DORA financial entity that is a GDPR controller satisfies both, with the classification-driven overlay from DORA adding cipher-strength tiering.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Y — the DORA financial entity is the GDPR controller on the same data assets.

**Downstream implication:** Stack-merge with overlay: DORA's classification-driven cipher-strength tiering (PR.DS-01 + GV.RM-04 per SR-DORA-003) sits on top of the joint at-rest encryption; for a DORA financial entity that is also a GDPR controller, a tiered key-management architecture addresses both.

**Objective.** Data held at rest in storage is protected against unauthorised
access through appropriate technical measures — primarily encryption with
appropriate key custody — calibrated to the risks identified in the entity's
risk assessment and commensurate with the state of the art.

**Considerations.**
- The four participating regulations converge on the same substantive obligation
  (risk-anchored encryption of data at rest) but each anchors it to a distinct
  threshold: GDPR's `appropriate` (risk-based, personal-data scope), NIS 2's
  `where appropriate` (broader NIS scope), CRA's `state of the art` (product-
  level, harmonised-standards floor — the strictest), and DORA's `high standards`
  (ICT-system-level).
- CRDA-deep verified all 6 pairs as SAME — a single encryption deployment with
  a joint risk-assessment artefact (joint Article 32 / Art. 21(2)(h) / Annex I
  (2)(e) / Art. 9(2) coverage) discharges all four obligations on the same
  datastore.
- For artefacts touching only one regulatory scope, only the triggered sub-SO
  applies.

#### Scope-overlap principle (operational). The high-level SO
(`SO-D-01.1.HL`) engages on a given datastore **if and only if ≥2
applicable regulations' scopes overlap on the same storage object**
(e.g. personal data on a CRA-regulated product deployed by a NIS 2
essential entity that is also a DORA financial entity — a
vertically-integrated manufacturer-controller on a financial-services
stack). Pure co-applicability of disjoint-scope regulations on different
artefacts does **not** trigger cross-regulation aggregation — see the
Activation logic in the file header. For a 5-FTE SaaS startup with
GDPR + CRA applicability but no NIS 2 / DORA applicability, only the
GDPR and CRA sub-SOs activate; the high-level SO engages on the same

#### Aggregation logic (from CRDA-deep verified classifications)

##### Emergent tensions (3+ regulations)
<!-- emergent: GDPR,NIS2,CRA,DORA -->

- All four regulations impose an at-rest confidentiality obligation, but the **threshold anchor** diverges: GDPR `appropriate` (risk-based), NIS 2 `where appropriate` (with appropriate-and-proportionate), CRA `state of the art` (harmonised-standards floor), DORA `high standards` (OJ-literal, no measurement criterion pending ESAs RTS). A DORA financial entity that is a NIS 2 essential entity AND processes personal data AND uses CRA-regulated products must reconcile four parallel thresholds; a Layer 2/3 analysis should establish whether the CRA state-of-the-art floor is automatically sufficient for the other three (likely yes for the at-rest dimension).
- **CRA "personal or other" scope extension** beyond GDPR's personal-data scope creates a compliance-population gap: a CRA manufacturer protecting non-personal data (e.g. sensor telemetry) may not be in GDPR scope, but a NIS 2 entity that ingests the same data is in scope. A CRA baseline met at the product level does not by itself discharge the NIS 2 entity's deployment-side responsibility.

<!-- /emergent -->

##### SR cross-validation
CSF mappings converge on PR.DS-01 (Data-at-rest protected) for all four regulations, with CRA additionally mapping to PR.DS-02 (in-transit) per the SR-CRA-001 umbrella and DORA additionally mapping to PR.DS-02/PR.DS-10/PR.DS-12 for the in-transit/in-use/risk-strategy dimensions. The shared PR.DS-01 anchor is the operational pivot; divergence is at the supplementary layer (CRA: harmonised standards; DORA: classification-driven key protection; GDPR: pseudonymisation/Art. 4(5) de-attribution; NIS 2: appropriate-and-proportionate). No contradictory CSF mappings observed.

##### Downstream implication
For Phase 1C Doc 07 (cross-regulation consolidation in the company profile), D-01.1 is a **stack-merge** sub-domain: a single at-rest control (e.g. disk encryption with key custody) typically satisfies all four regulators when documentation links each one. Proportionality: a 5-FTE SaaS may default to the CRA state-of-the-art floor as the highest common denominator; a 500-FTE financial entity must additionally document the DORA classification-driven cipher strength overlay.

<!-- participants: GDPR, NIS2, CRA, DORA; AI_Act absent -->

**Participants (from CRDA):** GDPR, NIS2, CRA, DORA (AI_Act is not present; AI Act §D-01.1 marks CRA Annex I (2)(e) as sole authority per taxonomy §4.1).

##### Pair: GDPR ↔ NIS2

**GDPR article (verbatim from SR-GDPR-001, Art. 5(1)(f) + Art. 32(1)(a)/(b)):**
> Art. 5(1)(f) integrity and confidentiality … processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data … protection against unauthorised or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction or damage, using appropriate technical or organisational measures. Art. 32(1)(b) the ability to ensure the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability and resilience of processing systems and services.

**NIS 2 article (verbatim from SR-NIS2-001, Art. 21(2)(h)):**
> Article 21(2)(h) of Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (NIS2) requires essential and important entities to establish policies and procedures regarding the use of cryptography and, where appropriate, encryption.

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: "personal data" (Art. 4(1) identifiability; pseudonymised data still in scope per CJEU Breyer and IAB Europe C-604/22).
- NIS 2 scope: "network and information systems" holding data that is "stored, transmitted or otherwise processed" (Art. 6(1)+(2)) — broader than personal data; includes operational, ICT-telemetry, business-confidential, and machine-generated data.
- Scope overlap: Y when the at-rest datastore holds personal data; Y (conditional) — typically the same storage holds both personal and non-personal data in modern stacks.

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: continuous obligation under Art. 5(1)(f) + Art. 32(1) (no event trigger; risk-anchored floor).
- NIS 2 trigger: continuous obligation under Art. 21(2)(h) + Art. 21(1) appropriate-and-proportionate.
- Trigger alignment: SAME (both continuous, both risk-anchored; no event-trigger divergence).

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: "appropriate" anchored to the five Art. 32(1) preamble factors (state of the art, costs, nature, scope, context, purposes, risks).
- NIS 2: "where appropriate" anchored to the same Art. 21(1) preamble plus the "appropriate and proportionate" qualifier (state of the art, standards, cost, exposure, size, likelihood, severity).
- Tension: NONE — the two thresholds converge on risk-anchored proportionality; the NIS 2 "where appropriate" hedge admits a narrower scope than GDPR's "appropriate", but where NIS 2 is narrower, GDPR is stricter (always applicable to personal data).

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: supervisory authority (Art. 58 enforcement); data subjects are notified on breach if Art. 34(1) high-risk threshold met (or carve-out under Art. 34(3)(a)).
- NIS 2: CSIRT or competent authority (Art. 23 reporting cascade); no direct data-subject notification.

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: technical-and-organis measures + accountability demonstrability under Art. 5(2).
- NIS 2: documented cryptography policy (SR-NIS2-001 title), with risk-anchored policy-and-procedure evidence.

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** COMPLEMENTARY.

**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** SAME (CORRECTED — terminology only, not substance).

**Reasoning (1-2 paragraphs):**
The OJ texts impose the same substantive obligation (encryption of data at rest where the risk assessment indicates) anchored to the same proportionality test (state of the art, risk factors). The two differ in (a) scope qualifier ("personal data" vs "network and information systems") — which makes NIS 2 broader; (b) anchor qualifier ("appropriate" vs "where appropriate") — which makes GDPR stricter for personal data; and (c) the GDPR-only existence of the Art. 34(3)(a) unintelligibility carve-out from data-subject notification. These are scope-depth and wording differences on a common obligation, not fundamentally different regulatory framings. CRDA's COMPLEMENTARY is consistent with this reading but understates the convergence: the two are best described as EQUIVALENT obligations on the same event (encryption at rest of personal data on a NIS 2 essential entity that is also a GDPR controller). The compliance population is the same party on the same artefact.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Y — the GDPR controller that is also a NIS 2 essential entity bears both obligations on the same datastore.

- If Y: the COMPLEMENTARY classification is correct; the practical reconciliation is stacked documentation (the GDPR Article 32 risk assessment doubles as the NIS 2 cryptography-policy risk rationale).

**Downstream implication (1-2 sentences):** For Phase 1C Doc 07, D-01.1 is a **stack-merge with documentation** sub-domain: a single at-rest encryption deployment (e.g. approved symmetric cipher with appropriate key custody under separate logical access) plus the joint Article 32 / Art. 21(2)(h) risk-assessment artefact discharges both obligations on a NIS 2 essential entity that is a GDPR controller.

## CRA v0.2 — Article 13 Obligations of Manufacturers (THE HEART)

> **Source.** OJ L, 20.11.2024, p. 34–37. `03_REFERENCE_MATERIAL/Regulatory_References/CRA.txt`, lines ~2177–2370.
>
> **Pattern.** Article quoted in full (paragraph by paragraph), then per-clause analysis. Universal `[recital NN]` convention applied.
>
> **Article 13 is the operational core of CRA** — 25 paragraphs, ~35 atomic clauses. It is the analog of GDPR Art. 32 and NIS 2 Art. 21. It defines the 6-phase product-lifecycle obligations (planning, design, development, production, delivery, maintenance), the cybersecurity risk assessment, the due-diligence duty on third-party components, the support period, the technical documentation, and the corrective-measures duty.

### 1. Article 13 — full text (key paragraphs)

> **Article 13 — Obligations of manufacturers**
>
> **1.** When placing a product with digital elements on the market, manufacturers shall ensure that it has been designed, developed and produced in accordance with the essential cybersecurity requirements set out in Part I of Annex I.

> **2.** For the purpose of complying with paragraph 1, manufacturers shall undertake an assessment of the cybersecurity risks associated with a product with digital elements and take the outcome of that assessment into account during the **planning, design, development, production, delivery and maintenance phases** of the product with digital elements with a view to minimising cybersecurity risks, preventing incidents and minimising their impact, **including in relation to the health and safety of users**.

> **3.** The cybersecurity risk assessment shall be documented and updated as appropriate during a support period to be determined in accordance with paragraph 8 of this Article. That cybersecurity risk assessment shall comprise at least an analysis of cybersecurity risks based on the **intended purpose and reasonably foreseeable use, as well as the conditions of use, of the product with digital elements**, such as the operational environment or the assets to be protected, taking into account the length of time the product is expected to be in use. The cybersecurity risk assessment shall indicate whether and, if so in what manner, the security requirements set out in Part I, point (2), of Annex I are applicable to the relevant product with digital elements and how those requirements are implemented as informed by the cybersecurity risk assessment. It shall also indicate how the manufacturer is to apply Part I, point (1), of Annex I and the vulnerability handling requirements set out in Part II of Annex I.

> **4.** When placing a product with digital elements on the market, the manufacturer shall include the cybersecurity risk assessment referred to in paragraph 3 of this Article in the technical documentation required pursuant to Article 31 and Annex VII. …

> **5.** For the purpose of complying with paragraph 1, manufacturers shall **exercise due diligence when integrating components sourced from third parties** so that those components do not compromise the cybersecurity of the product with digital elements, including when integrating components of free and open-source software that have not been made available on the market in the course of a commercial activity.

> **6.** Manufacturers shall, upon identifying a vulnerability in a component, including in an open source-component, which is integrated in the product with digital elements report the vulnerability to the person or entity manufacturing or maintaining the component, and **address and remediate** the vulnerability in accordance with the vulnerability handling requirements set out in Part II of Annex I. …

> **7.** The manufacturers shall systematically document, in a manner that is proportionate to the nature and the cybersecurity risks, **relevant cybersecurity aspects** concerning the products with digital elements, including vulnerabilities of which they become aware and any relevant information provided by third parties, and shall, where applicable, update the cybersecurity risk assessment of the products.

> **8.** Manufacturers shall ensure, when placing a product with digital elements on the market, and for the support period, that vulnerabilities of that product, including its components, are handled effectively and in accordance with the essential cybersecurity requirements set out in Part II of Annex I.
>
> Manufacturers shall determine the support period so that it reflects the length of time during which the product is expected to be in use, taking into account, in particular, **reasonable user expectations, the nature of the product, including its intended purpose**, as well as **relevant Union law determining the lifetime** of products with digital elements. When determining the support period, manufacturers may also take into account the support periods of products with digital elements offering a similar functionality placed on the market by other manufacturers, the availability of the operating environment, the support periods of integrated components that provide core functions and are sourced from third parties as well as relevant guidance provided by the dedicated administrative cooperation group (ADCO) established pursuant to Article 52(15) and the Commission. **The matters to be taken into account in order to determine the support period shall be considered in a manner that ensures proportionality.**
>
> Without prejudice to the second subparagraph, the support period shall be **at least five years**. Where the product with digital elements is expected to be in use for less than five years, the support period shall correspond to the expected use time.
>
> …
>
> **9.** Manufacturers shall ensure that each security update, as referred to in Part II, point (8), of Annex I, which has been made available to users during the support period, **remains available after it has been issued for a minimum of 10 years or for the remainder of the support period, whichever is longer**.

> **10.** Where a manufacturer has placed subsequent substantially modified versions of a software product on the market, that manufacturer may ensure compliance with the essential cybersecurity requirement set out in Part II, point (2), of Annex I only for the version that it has last placed on the market, provided that theusers of the versions that were previously placed on the market have access to the version last placed on the market free of charge and do not incur additional costs to adjust the hardware and software environment in which they use the original version of that product.

> **12.** Before placing a product with digital elements on the market, manufacturers shall draw up the technical documentation referred to in Article 31. They shall carry out the chosen conformity assessment procedures as referred to in Article 32 or have them carried out. Where compliance … has been demonstrated by that conformity assessment procedure, manufacturers shall draw up the EU declaration of conformity in accordance with Article 28 and affix the CE marking in accordance with Article 30.

> **13.** Manufacturers shall **keep the technical documentation and the EU declaration of conformity at the disposal of the market surveillance authorities for at least 10 years** after the product with digital elements has been placed on the market or for the support period, whichever is longer.

> **14.** Manufacturers shall ensure that procedures are in place for products with digital elements that are part of a series of production to remain in conformity with this Regulation. …

> **15.** Manufacturers shall ensure that their products with digital elements bear a **type, batch or serial number or other element allowing their identification** …

> **16.** Manufacturers shall indicate the **name, registered trade name or registered trademark of the manufacturer, and the postal address, email address or other digital contact details**, as well as, where applicable, the website where the manufacturer can be contacted, on the product with digital elements …

> **17.** For the purposes of this Regulation, manufacturers shall designate a **single point of contact** to enable users to communicate directly and rapidly with them, including in order to facilitate reporting on vulnerabilities of the product with digital elements. …

> **18.** Manufacturers shall ensure that products with digital elements are accompanied by the **information and instructions to the user set out in Annex II**, in paper or electronic form. …

> **19.** Manufacturers shall ensure that the **end date of the support period** referred to in paragraph 8, including at least the month and the year, is clearly and understandably specified at the time of purchase …

> **20.** Manufacturers shall either provide a copy of the EU declaration of conformity or a **simplified EU declaration of conformity** with the product with digital elements. …

### 1. Annex I — full text

#### Part II — Vulnerability handling requirements

> Manufacturers of products with digital elements shall:
>
> **(1)** identify and document vulnerabilities and components contained in products with digital elements, including by drawing up a **software bill of materials** in a commonly used and machine-readable format covering at the very least the **top-level dependencies** of the products;
>
> **(2)** in relation to the risks posed to products with digital elements, **address and remediate vulnerabilities without delay**, including by providing security updates; where technically feasible, new security updates shall be provided **separately from functionality updates**;
>
> **(3)** apply **effective and regular tests and reviews** of the security of the product with digital elements;
>
> **(4)** once a security update has been made available, **share and publicly disclose** information about fixed vulnerabilities, including a description of the vulnerabilities, information allowing users to identify the product with digital elements affected, the impacts of the vulnerabilities, their severity and clear and accessible information helping users to remediate the vulnerabilities; in duly justified cases, where manufacturers consider the security risks of publication to outweigh the security benefits, they may **delay making public information** regarding a fixed vulnerability until after users have been given the possibility to apply the relevant patch;
>
> **(5)** put in place and enforce a **policy on coordinated vulnerability disclosure**;
>
> **(6)** take measures to **facilitate the sharing of information** about potential vulnerabilities in their product with digital elements as well as in third-party components contained in that product, including by providing a **contact address** for the reporting of the vulnerabilities discovered in the product with digital elements;
>
> **(7)** provide for mechanisms to **securely distribute updates** for products with digital elements to ensure that vulnerabilities are fixed or mitigated in a timely manner and, where applicable for security updates, in an automatic manner;
>
> **(8)** ensure that, where security updates are available to address identified security issues, they are **disseminated without delay** and, unless otherwise agreed between a manufacturer and a business user in relation to a tailor-made product with digital elements, **free of charge**, accompanied by advisory messages providing users with the relevant information, including on potential action to be taken.

## NIS2

#### 2.1 Definition (1) `network and information system` (NIS2-D01)

**Source text:**

> **(1)** **`'network and information system'`** means:

> **(a)** an electronic communications network as defined in Article 2, point (1), of Directive (EU) 2018/1972;

> **(b)** any device or group of interconnected or related devices, one or more of which, pursuant to a programme, carry out automatic processing of digital data; or

> **(c)** digital data stored, processed, retrieved or transmitted by elements covered under points (a) and (b) for the purposes of their operation, use, protection and maintenance;

**Analysis.**

- **VAG — S2** — `related` (in (b)) is undefined. Does `related` mean physically-connected, logically-connected (shared protocol), or merely co-located? A device in the same rack that is not networked is "related" to the rack? The clause does not say. **Two readings:**
  - **R1 (broad):** any device in physical or logical proximity to a network device counts — captures operational technology (OT), IoT, building-management systems, badge readers.
  - **R2 (narrow):** `related` requires a logical connection (e.g. shared bus, MQTT broker, network gateway) — excludes isolated co-located devices.

  | # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
  |---|---|---|
  | R1 | `related` = physical/logical proximity (broad) | None in OJ; ENISA guidance tilts broad |
  | R2 | `related` = logical connection only (narrow) | None in OJ; cybersecurity-industry practice (NIS 2 implementation studies) tilts narrow |

  *Berry anchor:* §5.1 (`related` undefined).

- **POLY — S2** — `device` in (b) has at least three readings:
  - **R1:** hardware device (physical appliance).
  - **R2:** virtual device (VM, container).
  - **R3:** software-as-device (e.g. a software-defined gateway with no dedicated hardware).

  | # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
  |---|---|---|
  | R1 | `device` = hardware only | None in OJ |
  | R2 | `device` = hardware or virtual | None in OJ; Reg. 2019/881 uses "ICT product" with broader scope |
  | R3 | `device` = hardware, virtual, or software-only | None in OJ; CSA cloud computing definitions allow software-only services |

  *Berry anchor:* §3.3.1 (`device`).

- **COORD — S2** — (a) OR (b) OR (c) — three coordinated definitions by disjunction. The OR is inclusive (the chapeau says `means:` then lists (a), (b), (c) — consistent with EU drafting convention for inclusive definitions). However, **(c) is a data definition** while (a) and (b) are system/equipment definitions. A system can be (a) without containing (c); a data store can be (c) without being (a) or (b). The three readings:
  - **R1:** (a), (b), (c) are all independent paths — qualifying as any one suffices.
  - **R2:** (a) and (b) are system-level; (c) is a downstream qualifier — qualifying under (a) or (b) and also holding (c) is required.
  - **R3:** The Directive uses (a) for ECS-anchored networks, (b) for ICT-anchored systems, (c) for the data dimension; an entity qualifies if any one applies, but the obligations attach to all three jointly.

  | # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
  |---|---|---|
  | R1 | Independent OR — any one path qualifies | None in OJ; drafting convention |
  | R2 | (a)/(b) qualify the system; (c) qualifies the data inside the system | None in OJ; recital context |
  | R3 | Qualifying as any one path suffices; obligations attach jointly | None in OJ |

  *Berry anchor:* §5.4.7 (OR across three sub-clauses).

- **Layer 1 observation** — The GDPR analog (Art. 4(1) `personal data`) and the CRA analog (Art. 3(1) `product with digital elements`) both produce definitional-anchor problems downstream. NIS 2's `network and information system` is the operational scope-defining term of Chapter IV; every obligation in Art. 21–25 attaches to "essential and important entities" only in respect of "their" network and information systems. Resolving (1) determines the scope of all Chapter IV obligations.

#### 2.2 Definition (2) `security of network and information systems` (NIS2-D02)

**Source text:**

> **(2)** **`'security of network and information systems'`** means the ability of network and information systems to resist, at a given level of confidence, any event that may compromise the availability, authenticity, integrity or confidentiality of stored, transmitted or processed data or of the services offered by, or accessible via, those network and information systems;

**Analysis.**

- **VAG — S3** — `at a given level of confidence`: the modifier is open. The reading determines the threshold at which an entity's security measures are deemed sufficient. Three materially distinct readings:
  - **R1:** `level of confidence` is subjective/qualitative — management attestation.
  - **R2:** `level of confidence` is relative — comparable to peer entities or sector baseline.
  - **R3:** `level of confidence` is absolute — measurable (e.g. probability metric, sigma-level).

  | # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
  |---|---|---|
  | R1 | Subjective/qualitative (management attestation) | None in OJ |
  | R2 | Relative (peer/sector baseline) | None in OJ; ENISA security-baseline guidance |
  | R3 | Absolute (probabilistic metric) | None in OJ; ISO/IEC 27004 measurement-of-security concept |

  R1 and R3 produce materially distinct compliance obligations (R1 admits paper exercises; R3 requires measurement). R2 is intermediate. S3.

  *Berry anchor:* §5.1 (`level of confidence`).

- **POLY — S2** — `ability … to resist`: the metaphor of "resistance" implies a binary (resists/does not resist), but security is a continuous variable. The reading:
  - **R1:** Binary — the system either resists or does not.
  - **R2:** Threshold — the system resists all events up to a defined threshold.
  - **R3:** Probabilistic — the system resists with a defined probability.

  | # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
  |---|---|---|
  | R1 | Binary resistance | None in OJ |
  | R2 | Threshold resistance | None in OJ; ISO/IEC 27005 risk-threshold concept |
  | R3 | Probabilistic resistance | None in OJ; FAIR / NIST 800-30 quantitative-risk concept |

- **VAG — S2** — `authenticity`: GDPR Art. 5(1)(d) and ISO/IEC 27000 define `authenticity`; NIS 2 does not. A reading of `authenticity` requires recourse to the broader cybersecurity-vocabulary ecosystem (ISO/IEC 27000, NIST Glossary). The Directive does not import any of these.

- **COORD — S2** — `availability OR authenticity OR integrity OR confidentiality` (OR of four CIA-triad dimensions plus authenticity). The reading:
  - **R1:** Compromise of any one dimension triggers the security obligation.
  - **R2:** The four dimensions are the full CIA-A set; an entity is "secure" only if all four are protected.

  |---|---|---|
  | R1 | Any one compromise = security event | None in OJ; recital 32 (cybersecurity) |

- **Layer 1 observation** — The definition defines the **state of security**, not the **state of cybersecurity**. NIS 2(2) `security of network and information systems` is a broader concept than NIS 2(3) `cybersecurity`. (3) is a cross-reference to Reg. 2019/881. The boundary between "security of network and information systems" and "cybersecurity" in NIS 2 itself is not drawn in Art. 6 — the Directive uses both terms (e.g. Art. 1(2) "high common level of cybersecurity", Art. 21(1) "security of network and information systems"). A purely linguistic Layer 1 reading flags this terminological overlap for Layer 2 adjudication.

### 4. Cross-clause structural observations (Art. 25)

1. **Art. 25 is encouragement-only.** No binding entity obligation. The article's operational impact is on the entity's evidence base: standards cited in Art. 25(1) and ENISA guidelines produced under Art. 25(2) are non-mandatory but provide a soft path to Art. 21 compliance.

2. **The technology-neutrality clause is the most distinctive single phrase.** `without imposing or discriminating in favour of the use of a particular type of technology` is the only explicit technology-neutrality mandate in Chapter IV. It is a structural commitment to standards-based (not technology-specific) regulation.

3. **ENISA's `shall draw up advice and guidelines` is one of the few mandatory provisions on ENISA in Chapter IV.** Most ENISA references in Chapter IV are `may` (Art. 21(5), Art. 22(1), Art. 23(11), Art. 24(3)). Art. 25(2) is `shall draw up` — a positive obligation.

4. **Cross-regulation parallel.** Art. 25 mirrors the standardisation provisions in CRA Art. 27 (presumption of conformity via harmonised standards) and AI Act Art. 40 (conformity assessment via harmonised standards). The `European and international standards and technical specifications` triplet is the standard cross-regulation formulation.

#### 6.1 Art. 4 propagation pattern — three distinct proportionality regimes

Art. 4 establishes **three distinct proportionality regimes** that operate simultaneously:

- **Art. 4(1) — Ch. II proportionality** (CL4-1): default for the ICT risk management framework.
- **Art. 4(2) — Ch. III, IV, V Sec. I proportionality** (CL4-2): default for incident reporting, testing, and third-party risk.
- **Art. 4(3) — CA-review proportionality** (CL4-3): meta-provision requiring CAs to consider proportionality when reviewing the framework.

The three regimes are **not symmetric**: Art. 4(1) is self-implementing (the entity applies it directly); Art. 4(2) defers to chapter-specific clauses (Art. 16 simplification, Art. 25(3) microenterprise exception); Art. 4(3) is addressed to CAs.

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`principle of proportionality`); §5.1 (`proportionate`).
- **Structural implication:** the proportionality overlay is **inherited by every Ch. II–V Sec. I clause** in v0.2. Every clause card in `02b`–`05a` carries `proportionality-status: Art. 4 applies`. The v0.2 convention was established in the pilot file (`01_DORA_Art3_Definitions.md` §2 note on Art. 4).
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** Art. 4's proportionality pattern mirrors **NIS 2 Art. 1(2)** (proportionality in scope) and **CRA Art. 1(2)** (proportionality for microenterprises and SMEs). DORA Art. 4 is more elaborate than either, providing explicit CA-review (CL4-3) that NIS 2 and CRA do not have.

#### 6.3 Art. 6 chapeau — the framework's three-attribute triad

Art. 6(1) opens with `sound, comprehensive and well-documented` (three attributes, AND). This is the **framework-level analog** of:

- **Art. 24(1) `sound and comprehensive`** (testing programme, two attributes; CL24-1)
- **CRA Annex I Part I §1 chapeau** `products with digital elements shall be designed, developed and produced …` (manufacturing lifecycle, three verbs; CRA-CL14)
- **NIS 2 Art. 21(1) `appropriate and proportionate technical, operational and organisational measures`** (measures chapeau)

The 3-attribute triad (`sound, comprehensive and well-documented`) is **the most demanding framework chapeau in the 5-regulation corpus** — it requires three distinct qualities (effectiveness, completeness, traceability). CRA Annex I Part I §1 has three verbs (design, develop, produce) but they describe **processes** not qualities. DORA's triad describes **qualities of an artifact** (the framework itself), which is a different kind of obligation.

- **Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`sound`, `comprehensive`, `well-documented`); §5.4.7 (AND); §5.5 (parallelism).
- **Structural implication:** the framework's three attributes set the bar for every Ch. II–V Sec. I clause. Any clause that fails one of the three (e.g. a framework that is `comprehensive and well-documented` but not `sound`) would be non-compliant.
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** the triad is the most explicit framework-quality specification in the corpus. NIS 2 Art. 21(1) uses `appropriate and proportionate` (2 qualifiers); CRA Annex I Part I uses process verbs; GDPR Art. 24 uses `appropriate` (1 qualifier). DORA's 3-attribute triad is the most demanding.

#### 6.1 Art. 25(1) — 12-modality open list (POLY/S3)

Art. 25(1) enumerates 12 specific test modalities under the `such as …` open-list signal. The structural pattern is **identical to CRA Annex I Part I §2(d)** (`authentication, identity or access management systems` under `including but not limited to`) and to the CRA Annex I enumeration generally.

- **Berry anchor:** §5.1 `such as` open-list marker; same pattern as CRA-C05 (Annex I Part I §2(d)).
- **Structural implication:** the 12 modalities are illustrative, not exhaustive. Financial entities may add (e.g. red team, social engineering, fuzz testing) but should not omit any of the 12 without justification.
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** CRA, NIS 2 (Art. 21(2)(e) `secure system development and maintenance` includes similar open enumerations), and DORA all converge on the open-list pattern. This is a **regulatory-drafting convention**, not an artefact of any single regulation.

#### 6.5 Art. 25(2) — Sectoral carve-out for CSD/CCP

Art. 25(2) imposes a **pre-deployment vulnerability assessment obligation on two specific entity types** (CSDs and CCPs). This is a sectoral carve-out unique in Ch. IV — no other clause names specific entity types.

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`central securities depositories`, `central counterparties`).
- **Structural implication:** CSDs and CCPs are **financial market infrastructures (FMIs)** with systemic importance. The pre-deployment assessment obligation reflects the FMI-specific risk profile (a deployment-induced vulnerability could cascade to the entire clearing or settlement system).
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** CRA does not impose pre-deployment obligations. NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(f) requires `policies and procedures for cryptography` but not pre-deployment assessments. **DORA Art. 25(2) is a sectoral specificity.**
