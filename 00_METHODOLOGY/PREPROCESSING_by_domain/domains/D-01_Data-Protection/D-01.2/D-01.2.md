##### Pair: GDPR ↔ CRA

**GDPR article (verbatim — SR-GDPR-002, Art. 32(1)(b) + Art. 32(2)):**
> Art. 32(1)(b) the ongoing confidentiality, integrity, availability and resilience of processing systems and services. Art. 32(2) in assessing the appropriate level of security account shall be taken in particular of the risks … from accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data transmitted, stored or otherwise processed.

**CRA article (verbatim — SR-CRA-003, Annex I Part I (2)(e) + (2)(j)):**
> Annex I Part I (2)(e) … by encrypting relevant data at rest or in transit using state-of-the-art mechanisms. Annex I Part I (2)(j) limit attack surfaces … including external interfaces.

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR: personal data in transit (Art. 32(2) "transmitted").
- CRA: data in transit through product with digital elements (personal or other).
- Scope overlap: Y when the CRA product transmits personal data.

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR: continuous + on material change.
- CRA: continuous.
- Trigger alignment: SAME.

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: "appropriate" anchored to Art. 32(2) risk events.
- CRA: "state-of-the-art mechanisms".
- Tension: NONE.

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: supervisory authority.
- CRA: MSA + ENISA.

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: technical-and-organis measures + risk assessment of the five-event enumeration.
- CRA: transport-security profile in technical documentation (Annex VII §2(b)).

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** COMPLEMENTARY.

**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** SAME (CORRECTED — wording only).

**Reasoning:** The two impose the same in-transit confidentiality obligation on the same artefacts (channels transmitting personal data), with the same PR.DS-02 + PR.IR-01 CSF anchors. The CRA substrate is stricter (state-of-the-art) than GDPR's risk-anchored layer; meeting CRA discharges GDPR on the in-transit channel. **Scope-disjoint test:** Y when the same product transmits personal data; disjoint in baseline (CRA manufacturer ≠ GDPR controller).

**Downstream implication:** Stack-merge on the same channel; a CRA-compliant TLS configuration + network-segmentation profile discharges the GDPR Art. 32(1)(b)/(2) in-transit control.

  three obligations on the same channel.

  three encryption sub-domains and modern TLS configurations typically
  satisfy all three without dedicated additional investment.

##### Emergent tensions (3+ regulations)
<!-- emergent: GDPR,CRA,DORA -->

- All three regulations converge on PR.DS-02 as the operational anchor for in-transit confidentiality, but the **scope-determiner** diverges: GDPR (personal data), CRA (personal or other, product-stored/transmitted), DORA (ICT-supported data, financial-entity scope). A CRA-regulated product transmitting mixed personal + telemetry data to a DORA financial entity host running a GDPR-scoped controller processing the personal-data subset creates a three-way layered obligation with no clean joint-decision-rule; the Layer 1/2 synthesis should establish whether the CRA state-of-the-art in-transit primitive is automatically sufficient for the other two (likely yes for confidentiality; the integrity + authenticity layers of DORA require entity-level additional controls).

<!-- /emergent -->

##### SR cross-validation
All three regulations map to PR.DS-02 (Data-in-transit protected) + PR.IR-01 (Networks protected from unauthorized logical access / usage). The shared anchor means a single network-security design typically discharges the three obligations. CRA additionally maps to PR.IR-01 for the Annex I (2)(j) attack-surface-limitation leg; GDPR additionally maps to PR.IR-01 for the Art. 32(1)(b) network-layer support. No contradictory mappings observed.

##### Downstream implication
For Phase 1C Doc 07, D-01.2 is a **single-control** sub-domain: a mutual-TLS + network-segmentation design typically satisfies all three regulators for an in-scope company. Proportionality: in-transit is universally the cheapest of the three encryption sub-domains; a 5-FTE SaaS with modern TLS configurations will satisfy all three without dedicated additional investment.

### 1. Annex I — full text

#### Part II — Vulnerability handling requirements

> Manufacturers of products with digital elements shall:
>
> **(1)** identify and document vulnerabilities and components contained in products with digital elements, including by drawing up a **software bill of materials** in a commonly used and machine-readable format covering at the very least the **top-level dependencies** of the products;
>
> **(2)** in relation to the risks posed to products with digital elements, **address and remediate vulnerabilities without delay**, including by providing security updates; where technically feasible, new security updates shall be provided **separately from functionality updates**;
>
> **(3)** apply **effective and regular tests and reviews** of the security of the product with digital elements;
>
> **(4)** once a security update has been made available, **share and publicly disclose** information about fixed vulnerabilities, including a description of the vulnerabilities, information allowing users to identify the product with digital elements affected, the impacts of the vulnerabilities, their severity and clear and accessible information helping users to remediate the vulnerabilities; in duly justified cases, where manufacturers consider the security risks of publication to outweigh the security benefits, they may **delay making public information** regarding a fixed vulnerability until after users have been given the possibility to apply the relevant patch;
>
> **(5)** put in place and enforce a **policy on coordinated vulnerability disclosure**;
>
> **(6)** take measures to **facilitate the sharing of information** about potential vulnerabilities in their product with digital elements as well as in third-party components contained in that product, including by providing a **contact address** for the reporting of the vulnerabilities discovered in the product with digital elements;
>
> **(7)** provide for mechanisms to **securely distribute updates** for products with digital elements to ensure that vulnerabilities are fixed or mitigated in a timely manner and, where applicable for security updates, in an automatic manner;
>
> **(8)** ensure that, where security updates are available to address identified security issues, they are **disseminated without delay** and, unless otherwise agreed between a manufacturer and a business user in relation to a tailor-made product with digital elements, **free of charge**, accompanied by advisory messages providing users with the relevant information, including on potential action to be taken.

#### 4.1 CL4-1 — Art. 4(1) — Proportionality chapeau (Ch. II)

**Source locus:** Art. 4(1).

> Financial entities shall implement the rules laid down in Chapter II in accordance with the principle of proportionality, taking into account their size and overall risk profile, and the nature, scale and complexity of their services, activities and operations.

**Instance 1 — VAG — S3 — `principle of proportionality`**

The proportionality principle is a **general principle of EU law** (case-law-laden: Cassis de Dijon, Lange, Schwarz, etc.) but Art. 4(1) makes it the **specific override for Ch. II implementation**. Three materially distinct readings:

- **R1:** Quantitative (proportional to size: headcount, balance-sheet total, turnover).
- **R2:** Risk-based (proportional to the ICT risk profile of the entity).
- **R3:** Combined: size, risk profile, AND nature/scale/complexity (literal reading).

| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Quantitative (size) | None in OJ |
| R2 | Risk-based | In-Directive: literal `overall risk profile` |
| R3 | Combined (size + risk + nature/scale/complexity) | In-Directive: literal `taking into account their size and overall risk profile, and the nature, scale and complexity` |

R1 vs R3 admit materially distinct compliance populations (R1: small entity is automatically exempted; R3: a small entity with high risk profile may still need to comply fully). S3.

*Berry anchor:* §5.1 (`proportionality`); §3.3.1 (`principle of proportionality`).

**Instance 2 — VAG — S3 — `nature, scale and complexity`**

The three-factor qualifier. Each term admits its own ambiguity.

#### 6.1 Art. 4 propagation pattern — three distinct proportionality regimes

Art. 4 establishes **three distinct proportionality regimes** that operate simultaneously:

- **Art. 4(1) — Ch. II proportionality** (CL4-1): default for the ICT risk management framework.
- **Art. 4(2) — Ch. III, IV, V Sec. I proportionality** (CL4-2): default for incident reporting, testing, and third-party risk.
- **Art. 4(3) — CA-review proportionality** (CL4-3): meta-provision requiring CAs to consider proportionality when reviewing the framework.

The three regimes are **not symmetric**: Art. 4(1) is self-implementing (the entity applies it directly); Art. 4(2) defers to chapter-specific clauses (Art. 16 simplification, Art. 25(3) microenterprise exception); Art. 4(3) is addressed to CAs.

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`principle of proportionality`); §5.1 (`proportionate`).
- **Structural implication:** the proportionality overlay is **inherited by every Ch. II–V Sec. I clause** in v0.2. Every clause card in `02b`–`05a` carries `proportionality-status: Art. 4 applies`. The v0.2 convention was established in the pilot file (`01_DORA_Art3_Definitions.md` §2 note on Art. 4).
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** Art. 4's proportionality pattern mirrors **NIS 2 Art. 1(2)** (proportionality in scope) and **CRA Art. 1(2)** (proportionality for microenterprises and SMEs). DORA Art. 4 is more elaborate than either, providing explicit CA-review (CL4-3) that NIS 2 and CRA do not have.
