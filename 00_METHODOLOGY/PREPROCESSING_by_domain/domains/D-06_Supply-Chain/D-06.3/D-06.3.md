##### Pair: GDPR ↔ NIS2

**GDPR article (verbatim, Art. 28(3)(a)–(h)):**
> "Processing by a processor … be governed by a contract … that sets out … the subject-matter and duration of the processing, the nature and purpose of the processing, the type of personal data and categories of data subjects, and the obligations and rights of the controller. That contract or other legal act shall stipulate, in particular, that the processor: (a) processes the personal data only on documented instructions from the controller …; (b) ensures that persons authorised to process the personal data have committed themselves to confidentiality …; (c) implements all the measures required pursuant to Article 32; (d) engages another processor only with the prior specific or general written authorisation of the controller …; (e) assists the controller in fulfilling its obligation to respond to requests for exercising the data subject's rights …; (f) assists the controller in ensuring compliance with the obligations pursuant to Articles 32 to 36; (g) at the choice of the controller, deletes or returns all the personal data to the controller after the end of the provision of services relating to processing … and deletes existing copies; (h) makes available to the controller all information necessary to demonstrate compliance with the obligations laid down in this Article and allow for and contribute to audits, including inspections, conducted by the controller or another auditor mandated by the controller." — Art. 28(3)(a)–(h) GDPR

**NIS 2 article (verbatim, Art. 21(2)(d) + Art. 21(3) sentence 1):**
> "… supply chain security including security-related aspects concerning the relationships between each entity and its direct suppliers or service providers." — NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(d)

**DORA article (verbatim, Art. 30(1)+(2)):**
> "The rights and obligations of the financial entity and of the ICT third-party service provider shall be clearly allocated and set out in writing … The contractual arrangements shall include, at least, the following elements: (a) a clear description of all functions and ICT services to be provided by the ICT third-party service provider … (b) the locations where the contracted and subcontracted functions and ICT services are to be provided and where the data are to be processed, including the storage location, and an obligation for the ICT third-party service provider to notify the financial entity in advance if it intends to change those locations; (c)) provisions on the availability, authenticity, integrity and confidentiality of the data, including the protection of data during transmission and processing; (d) provisions on ensuring access, recovery and return of the personal and non-personal data of the financial entity in the case of insolvency, resolution or termination of the ICT third-party service provider; (e) service level descriptions, including updates and revisions thereto; (f) the obligation of the ICT third-party service provider to provide assistance to the financial entity in the event of an ICT-related incident … at no extra cost or at a cost determined ex ante; (g) the obligation of the ICT third-party service provider to cooperate fully with the competent authority and the resolution authorities of the financial entity; (h) termination rights and related minimum notice periods for the financial entity … (i) conditions for participation of the ICT third-party service provider in the ICT security awareness programmes and digital operational resilience training of the financial entity …" — DORA Art. 30(1)+(2)(a)–(i)

(OJ-corrective note: per DORA validation report v0.2 F-10, the `audit rights` and `exit strategies` clauses that were originally attributed to Art. 30(2) are in fact in Art. 30(3)(e)(i) (audit rights) and Art. 30(3)(f) (exit strategies), both CIF-only. **Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: processor contract (controller ↔ processor).
- DORA scope: ICT third-party contract (financial entity ↔ ICT provider).
- Scope overlap: **Y when the financial entity is also a controller and the ICT provider is also a processor**.

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: per-processor contract.
- DORA trigger: per-ICT-third-party contract.
- Trigger alignment: **PARTIAL** — both per-contract; DORA adds explicit CIF-only clauses at Art. 30(3).

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: closed 8-element list.
- DORA: closed 9-element list (Art. 30(2)) + CIF-only clauses (Art. 30(3)).
- Tension: **GENUINE OVERLAP, NO CONTRADICTION** — the two lists overlap but are not identical. The closed AND-list reading of both lists means the union of all required clauses must appear in the contract. There is no clause in either list that contradicts a clause in the other — they are additive.

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: DPA.
- DORA: competent authority.

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: 8-element DPA.
- DORA: 9-element Art. 30(2) contract + CIF-only Art. 30(3) clauses (audit rights, exit strategies).

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** CONTRADICTORY (overlap)
**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** **CORRECTED — COMPLEMENTARY** (clause-list union)
**Reasoning:** GDPR Art. 28(3) has 8 mandatory elements; DORA Art. 30(2) has 9 minimum elements. The two lists overlap but are not identical. The CRDA reasoning itself acknowledges this: "the contract must satisfy both sets" — which is the textbook union pattern. There is no mutually exclusive obligation. The "contradictory" label is scope-incorrect; the correct label is "complementary" with consolidation requirement (union of clauses). When a financial entity contracts with a processor that is also an ICT third-party service provider, the contract must satisfy both sets — the union is the operative rule. Phase 2 must produce a consolidated clause matrix that satisfies the union without conflict.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? **Y when the financial entity is also a controller and the ICT provider is also a processor — both apply on the same supplier relationship.** The union rule applies.

**Downstream implication (Phase 1C Doc 07):** Phase 2 obligation derivation must produce a consolidated clause matrix. The intersection of GDPR Art. 28(3) and DORA Art. 30(2) is the operational hinge: financial entities that process personal data via ICT third-party providers must satisfy both clause sets. Phase 3 case generation must validate that supplier contracts in case companies include all required elements per the consolidated matrix. The CRDA "contradictory" label should be corrected to "complementary" with union rule.

#### Cross-recipient map

D-06.3 generates recipient fragmentation at the **contractual evidence** layer. **GDPR** evidence is addressed to the supervisory authority (DPA) through Art. 5(2) accountability, Art. 28(3) DPA contracts, and Chapter V transfer safeguards. **NIS 2** evidence is addressed to the competent authority or CSIRT through the supplier-risk register, direct-supplier contract terms, Art. 21(3) three-prong supplier assessment, and Art. 22 coordinated-assessment ingestion. **CRA** evidence is addressed to market surveillance authorities through importer/distributor pre-market verification, non-conformity escalation, and conformity-documentation retention. **DORA** evidence is addressed to the competent authority and, where applicable, resolution authorities through the Art. 28(5) standards record, Art. 30(2) contract elements, and CIF-only Art. 30(3) clauses.

#### Scope-overlap principle (operational)

The high-level SO (`SO-D-06.3.HL`) engages when ≥2 participating regulations apply to the same supplier relationship or to a single integrated supply-chain event. The dominant high-overlap scenario is a financial entity that is also a GDPR controller and a NIS 2 entity contracting with an ICT provider that processes personal data: GDPR Art. 28(3), NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(d)/(3), and DORA Art. 30 all apply, and the contract must satisfy the union of clauses. CRA usually operates on the product-market-access side rather than the contract itself; it joins the same architecture when the ICT service includes a CRA-classified product supplied through importer/distributor channels or when a component vulnerability is identified during the lifecycle.

#### Emergent tensions (from CRDA-deep §D-06.3)

**1 conditional emergent tension**: the **4-track contract architecture**. GDPR Art. 28(3) imposes an eight-element processor-contract list; DORA Art. 30(2) imposes a nine-element ICT-contract list and CIF-only Art. 30(3) clauses; NIS 2 imposes direct-supplier security and a three-prong supplier assessment; CRA imposes importer/distributor pre-market and non-conformity obligations through a market-surveillance channel. CRDA-deep confirms that this is not a true contradiction. The correct operational rule is clause-list union, with CIF-only DORA clauses scoped to critical or important functions and with GDPR-specific processor clauses retained whenever the ICT provider processes personal data.

A company with ≥2 applicable regulations in D-06.3 must maintain a contractual-security matrix keyed by party, role, product/service, and trigger. For a financial-entity-controller contracting with an ICT provider that is also a processor, the matrix includes GDPR Art. 28(3) + DORA Art. 30(2) + applicable Art. 30(3) CIF clauses, with NIS 2 satisfied by reference only where the financial entity is also a NIS 2 entity and the supplier is within the `direct` scope. For a CRA product supply chain, the matrix includes importer/distributor verification and escalation records rather than treating CRA as a DPA-like contract. The one emergent tension is resolved by preserving this multi-track matrix rather than by choosing a single stricter regime.

##### Emergent tensions (3+ regulations)
<!-- emergent: GDPR,NIS2,CRA,DORA -->

- **4-track contract architecture with overlapping but non-identical clause lists.** GDPR Art. 28(3) = 8 elements; DORA Art. 30(2) = 9 elements; NIS2 Art. 21(3) = 3-prong supplier assessment; CRA Art. 19/20 = importer/distributor pre-market verification. Phase 2 must produce a consolidated supplier-contract template that operationalises the union of all four clause sets.

<!-- /emergent -->

##### SR cross-validation
NIST CSF 2.0 anchoring is dominated by **GV.SC-03** (Contracts with suppliers and third-party partners) and **GV.SC-04** (Suppliers and third-party partners are routinely assessed). ##### Downstream implication
Phase 2 obligation derivation must produce a consolidated contract-clause matrix. The intersection of GDPR Art. 28(3) and DORA Art. 30(2) is the operational hinge: financial entities that process personal data via ICT third-party providers must satisfy both clause sets. ##### Art. 5(1)(c) — Data minimisation

**Clause: GDPR-CL03** | type: principle | obligatedParty: CONTROLLER | obligationType: CONTINUOUS

**Berry anchor:** §3.3.5 (vagueness), §5.1 (`adequate`, `relevant`, `necessary`).

**Instance 1 — VAG / S3 / `adequate`, `relevant`, `necessary`**

Three inquiry-resistant adjectives; no measurement criterion. Art. 5(2) accountability shifts burden of demonstration to controller; Art. 35(7)(b) DPIA requires necessity assessment.

**Variant readings:**
| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Adequate iff it serves the designed purpose; necessity presumed by default. | None in OJ; industry self-regulation. |
| R2 | Adequate iff proportionate to purpose; demonstrable ex ante. | Art. 5(2) accountability; Art. 35(7)(b) DPIA. |
| R3 | Adequate iff least-intrusive among equivalent means under Art. 52(1) Charter necessity test. | CJEU C-62/14 Gasket; Charter Art. 52(1). |

R1 vs R3 yield materially distinct compliance obligations (R1 permits default collection patterns; R3 requires per-field justification).

**Instance 2 — SCOPE-Q / S2 / `purposes` (plural)**

`purposes` is plural — the minimisation test applies to each purpose. Berry §5.2.1 (plural-scope `purposes`). S2 because Art. 5(2) accountability and Art. 35(7)(b) DPIA partially anchor the reading.

### Article 22 — Automated individual decision-making

#### Verbatim (with highlighting)

> **Article 22**
> **Automated individual decision-making, including profiling**

> 1. The data subject shall have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her.

#### Recurring patterns

- `appropriate` and `adequate` appear in 5 of 10 clauses — the Chapter's twin Berry-flagged qualifiers.
- `compelling legitimate interests` (Art. 49) propagates from Art. 21(1) — cross-chapter POLY.
- Schrems II is the dominant interpretive anchor for Chapter V but is non-textual.

#### Cross-clause structural observations

1. **Art. 44 is the umbrella principle.** All transfers (Art. 45–49) must comply with Chapter V conditions AND with Art. 44's `not undermined` outcome obligation.
2. **Art. 45–46 are the primary mechanisms.** Adequacy (Art. 45) and safeguards (Art. 46) cover the majority of legitimate transfers. Art. 49 derogations are narrow last-resort exceptions.
3. **Schrems II decision cascade.** The Schrems II (C-311/18) ruling invalidated Privacy Shield, requiring SCCs to be supplemented with case-by-case transfer impact assessments. This non-textual standard shapes Chapter V application.
4. **`essentially equivalent` test vs `appropriate safeguards` standard.** Schrems II uses `essentially equivalent` for adequacy assessment; Art. 46(1) uses `appropriate safeguards`. The relationship between the two standards is judicially established but textually ambiguous.
5. **Art. 49(1)(a) consent three-way inconsistency.** Art. 4(11) `unambiguous` + Art. 9(2)(a) `explicit` + Art. 49(1)(a) `explicitly` — three different consent qualifiers for three different consent scenarios. Layer 2 would resolve.

#### Anti-pattern notes for synthesis

- Chapter V is the only chapter whose compliance depends on non-OJ-text anchors (Schrems II). The drafter's intent is sometimes inferred from CJEU jurisprudence rather than from the regulation text.
- Cross-regulation: DORA Art. 28(8) ICT third-party risk management overlaps with GDPR Chapter V for financial entities transferring data to non-EU ICT providers.

| R2 | Member-State-discretionary boundary | In-Directive: Art. 3(1)(e), (g) — Member States may identify additional essential entities |
| R3 | Annex I/II sector lists are exhaustive | In-Directive: Art. 2(1) + Annex I/II |

R1 is the structural reading. R2 is the Member-State-discretion reading. R3 is the sectoral-exhaustive reading. S2 because the surface ambiguity drives whether an entity falls in the scope of Art. 20 (R1/R2/R3 may give different answers for borderline cases like cloud-computing providers with mixed sectoral exposure).

*Berry anchor:* §3.3.1 (`essential and important entities`); §5.2.1 (Annex lists).

**Layer 1 observation** — The Art. 3 classification of an entity is a **prerequisite** for all of Art. 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25 obligations. If an entity is not classified as essential or important, none of these apply. The classification itself is ambiguous (R2: Member-State discretion; R3: Annex I/II exhaustive). This is the **single most important scope-defining layer of NIS 2** — flagged in synthesis.

---

### 4. Cross-clause structural observations (Art. 20)

1. **Art. 20(1) is the only direct personal-liability provision in NIS 2.** No other article in Chapter IV imposes liability on natural persons. The provision is short but materially consequential. National implementations have begun diverging (e.g. Belgium's transposition law, France's transposition law) on which liability types attach.
2. **Art. 20(2) creates a two-tier training obligation:** mandatory (for members) + encouraged (for employees). The asymmetry is intentional in the Directive; the operational impact is that entities may be judged on management-body training compliance but not on employee training.
3. **The management-body training obligation is the second cross-regulation `training` polysemy hotspot** (the first was GDPR Art. 39 DPO training; the third is CRA Annex I Part II §2(c) `security update procedures` which includes training). All three share the same POLY pattern.
4. **The `essential and important entities` classification propagates through every Chapter IV clause.** Resolving it is the prerequisite for any other finding.

#### 3.1 NIS2-CL07 — Art. 21(1) sentence 1 — Risk-management core obligation

**Source locus:**

#### 6.1 Art. 4 propagation pattern — three distinct proportionality regimes

Art. 4 establishes **three distinct proportionality regimes** that operate simultaneously:

- **Art. 4(1) — Ch. II proportionality** (CL4-1): default for the ICT risk management framework.
- **Art. 4(2) — Ch. III, IV, V Sec. I proportionality** (CL4-2): default for incident reporting, testing, and third-party risk.
- **Art. 4(3) — CA-review proportionality** (CL4-3): meta-provision requiring CAs to consider proportionality when reviewing the framework.

The three regimes are **not symmetric**: Art. 4(1) is self-implementing (the entity applies it directly); Art. 4(2) defers to chapter-specific clauses (Art. 16 simplification, Art. 25(3) microenterprise exception); Art. 4(3) is addressed to CAs.

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`principle of proportionality`); §5.1 (`proportionate`).
- **Structural implication:** the proportionality overlay is **inherited by every Ch. II–V Sec. I clause** in v0.2. Every clause card in `02b`–`05a` carries `proportionality-status: Art. 4 applies`. The v0.2 convention was established in the pilot file (`01_DORA_Art3_Definitions.md` §2 note on Art. 4).
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** Art. 4's proportionality pattern mirrors **NIS 2 Art. 1(2)** (proportionality in scope) and **CRA Art. 1(2)** (proportionality for microenterprises and SMEs). DORA Art. 4 is more elaborate than either, providing explicit CA-review (CL4-3) that NIS 2 and CRA do not have.

#### 6.4 Art. 19(6) — 5-recipient multi-supervisory routing

Art. 19(6) requires the competent authority to forward the incident details to **5 categories of recipients**:

| # | Recipient | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | EBA, ESMA or EIOPA | All |
| (b) | ECB | If Art. 2(1)(a)/(b)/(d) entities (credit institutions, insurance undertakings, investment firms) |
| (c) | NIS 2 CAs / SPOCs / CSIRTs | Cross-Directive (DORA ↔ NIS 2) |
| (d) | Resolution authorities (BRRD) + SRB | If incident poses risk to BRRD critical functions |
| (e) | Other public authorities under national law | Member State-determined |

This is the **most extensive multi-supervisory routing in the 5-regulation corpus**. An incident may simultaneously engage 5 different supervisory streams. The CA→recipients timing is `in a timely manner` (CL19-6 Instance 1, S3).

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`based, as applicable, on their respective competences`); §5.1 (`in a timely manner`).
- **Structural implication:** the CA-side reporting system must support **5-way multiplexing**. The financial-entity-side report (Art. 19(4)) produces a single document; the CA-side multiplexer splits it.
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** the CER Directive (EU) 2022/2557 parallel is **NIS 2 Art. 23(10)** (CSIRTs/CAs provide info to CER authorities). DORA's Art. 19(6)(d) (resolution authorities + SRB) is unique to DORA — no other regulation has a single-actor forwarding to the **Single Resolution Board**.

#### 6.6 Art. 22 — supervisory feedback: discretionary vs. mandatory

Art. 22(1) specifies that the competent authority **may, where feasible, provide in a timely manner relevant and proportionate feedback or high-level guidance**. The `may` is the operative trigger. Three readings (CL22-1 Instance 1): R1 mandatory, R2 discretionary (literal), R3 default-with-exceptions. R2 is the literal reading.

The structural pattern is **mandatory acknowledge + discretionary substance** — the CA must acknowledge receipt (per the `shall … acknowledge receipt and may … provide` coordination) but the substantive feedback is at CA discretion. This is **the opposite** of the NIS 2 Art. 23(5) pattern (mandatory CSIRT response within 24h), and is a deliberate DORA choice reflecting the CA-specific regulatory authority.

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`may`); §5.1 (`feasible`, `relevant`, `proportionate`).
- **Structural implication:** Art. 22(1) creates a **discretion gap** between reporting (mandatory, Art. 19) and supervisory response (discretionary, Art. 22). This is a **closed-loop asymmetry**: entities must report; CAs may respond.

#### 6.9 Art. 19(5) — outsourcing + responsibility clause

Art. 19(5) permits the financial entity to **outsource** the reporting obligations under Art. 19 to a third-party service provider (under Union and national sectoral law), **with the financial entity remaining fully responsible**. This mirrors DORA Art. 26(3) `retain at all times full responsibility for ensuring compliance with this Regulation` (for TLPT) and DORA Art. 22(1) `Without prejudice to the supervisory feedback received, financial entities shall remain fully responsible`.

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`in accordance with`); §5.1 (`fully responsible`).
- **Structural implication:** the **responsibility non-delegation principle** is consistent across Ch. III (Art. 19(5)), Ch. IV (Art. 26(3)), and Art. 22(1). A financial entity cannot outsource its reporting compliance by outsourcing the reporting process.
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** same pattern as GDPR Art. 28(4) (processor obligations do not discharge controller obligations) and NIS 2 Art. 21(1) (management-body responsibility).

#### 4.9 CL26-1 — Art. 26(1) — TLPT cadence

**Source locus:** Art. 26(1).

> Financial entities, other than entities referred to in Article 16(1), first subparagraph, and other than microenterprises, which are identified in accordance with paragraph 8, third subparagraph, of this Article, shall carry out **at least every 3 years** advanced testing by means of TLPT. #### 6.2 Art. 26(1) — Hard numeric anchor (3 years) reduces temporal ambiguity

The `at least every 3 years` cadence is a **hard numeric anchor**. This is the same Berry-relief pattern as CRA Annex I §2(c) `automatic security updates installed within an appropriate timeframe` (no anchor) vs. CRA Annex II §4 `disseminated without delay` (no anchor).

- **Berry anchor:** §5.7 (temporal operators); §5.1 (`at least every 3 years`).
- **Structural implication:** the 3-year cadence materially reduces temporal ambiguity for TLPT (compared to the year-by-year discretionary `at least yearly` of Art. 24(6), which is S2). However, the CA discretion clause (`reduce or increase this frequency`) partially reopens the ambiguity (CL26-1 Instance 2, S3).
- **Layer 1 observation:** hard numeric anchors are **the single most effective disambiguation technique** in EU cybersecurity regulations. DORA uses two of them in Ch. IV: 3 years (Art. 26(1)) and yearly (Art. 24(6)). Both anchor the temporal operator; the 3-year anchor is more rigid and less vulnerable to entity discretion.
