##### Pair: GDPR ↔ NIS2

**GDPR article (verbatim, Art. 28(1) + Art. 28(2) + Art. 4(7)/(8)):**
> "The controller shall use only processors providing sufficient guarantees to implement appropriate technical and organisational measures in such a manner that processing will meet the requirements of this Regulation and ensure the protection of data subjects' rights." — Art. 28(1) GDPR
>
> "The processor shall not engage another processor without prior specific or general written authorisation of the controller. In the case of general written authorisation, the processor shall inform the controller of any intended changes concerning the addition or replacement of other processors, thereby giving the controller the opportunity to object to such changes." — Art. 28(2) GDPR

**NIS 2 article (verbatim, Art. 21(2)(d)):**
> "Member States shall ensure that essential and important entities take appropriate and proportionate technical, operational and organisational measures to manage the risks posed to the security of network and information systems which those entities use for their activities or for the provision of their services, and to prevent or minimise the impact of incidents on recipients of their services and on other services. Taking into account the state of the art … those measures shall include, in particular … supply chain security including security-related aspects concerning the relationships between each entity and its direct suppliers or service providers." — NIS 2 Art. 21(1) + Art. 21(2)(d)

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: processors (data-processing entities).
- NIS 2 scope: direct suppliers and service providers (broader, includes non-data-processing suppliers).
- Scope overlap: **Y when the supplier is both a processor and a service provider** (typical in ICT outsourcing).

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: per-processor engagement.
- NIS 2 trigger: continuous supply-chain security.
- Trigger alignment: **PARTIAL** — GDPR is per-processor; NIS 2 is continuous + per-supplier.

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: qualitative "sufficient guarantees" — EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 factors.
- NIS 2: "appropriate and proportionate" (Art. 21(1) chapeau); "direct" scope-limiter (Art. 21(2)(d)).
- Tension: **NONE** — both are risk-anchored; GDPR's `sufficient guarantees` is a sharper threshold on the same risk substrate.

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: supervisory authority (DPA).
- NIS 2: CSIRT or competent authority.

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: written due-diligence questionnaire, certifications review, Art. 28(3) clauses.
- NIS 2: documented supply-chain security programme; Member State transposition may specify minimum-clause catalogues.

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** COMPLEMENTARY
**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** **SAME — COMPLEMENTARY**
**Reasoning:** GDPR due diligence is on the *processor* (data-processing entity); NIS 2 supply chain security is on *direct suppliers and service providers* (broader scope, includes non-data-processing suppliers). When the supplier is both a processor and a service provider, both apply; the GDPR `sufficient guarantees` test and the NIS 2 `direct` scope-limiter are layered, not overlapping. The NIS 2 `direct` SCOPE-Q S3 ambiguity (R1 Tier-1 / R2 technically integrated / R3 operationally dependent) is the key operational handle — see SR-NIS2-020 `ambiguity_notes` for the full 3-reading discussion.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? **Y when the controller and the essential / important entity is the same party** (typical for financial entities that are also essential entities under NIS 2). The two obligations stack on the same supplier relationship.

**Downstream implication (Phase 1C Doc 07):** Phase 2 obligation derivation must produce a layered supplier-assessment matrix: (1) GDPR processor due diligence (Art. 28(1)) covering all 4 EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 factors; (2) NIS 2 direct-supplier security under Art. 21(2)(d) with the chosen `direct` reading documented. Phase 3 case generation must validate the dual-layer assessment in case companies.

#### Validated relationships (from CRDA-deep §D-06.1)

Of 6 pairs, **6 verified as SAME (all CRDA initial classifications held after OJ-text analysis): 2 COMPLEMENTARY + 3 DIFFERENT-PERSPECTIVE + 1 EQUAL** — **no CORRECTED, no SCOPE-DISJOINT, no CONTRADICTORY** classifications on the vendor-risk-assessment duty itself. The four regulations look at supply chain from four distinct perspectives and are layered rather than contradictory:

- **GDPR ↔ NIS2** — COMPLEMENTARY (SAME): GDPR processor due diligence is on the *processor as legal entity* (data-processing counterparty); NIS 2 supply chain security is on *direct suppliers and service providers* (broader scope, includes non-data-processing suppliers per Art. 21(2)(d)). When the supplier is both a processor and a service provider, both apply and the GDPR `sufficient guarantees` test is a **sharper threshold** on the same risk substrate that NIS 2's `appropriate and proportionate` Art. 21(1) chapeau operates on. The NIS 2 `direct` scope-limiter (POLY-S3 with three readings — R1 Tier-1 only, R2 Tier-1 + technically integrated, R3 Tier-1 + operationally dependent — per `SR-NIS2-020` `ambiguity_notes`) is the key operational handle on which parts of the supplier base are in NIS 2 scope. There is **no contradiction** between GDPR's per-processor due diligence and NIS 2's continuous supply-chain security — they are layered, with the GDPR leg providing the data-processing-counterparty dimension and the NIS 2 leg providing the broader entity-level supply-chain dimension.
- **GDPR ↔ CRA** — DIFFERENT-PERSPECTIVE (SAME): GDPR processor due diligence is on the *processor as legal entity*; CRA third-party component due diligence is on *components as technical artefacts* (including non-commercial OSS, which GDPR does not separately contemplate per Art. 3(48) FOSS definition). When a CRA-compliant product is integrated into a GDPR controller's processing, the manufacturer has already done CRA-level component due diligence; the controller still owes GDPR-level processor due diligence on the **manufacturer** (the manufacturer is the processor when the controller deploys the product for processing). The two layers are different perspectives on the same supply chain — entity-level (GDPR) versus product-level (CRA). There is **no contradiction** because the two objects (processor as legal entity / component as technical artefact) and the two triggers (per-processor engagement / per-component integration) are distinct; the obligations stack vertically on the integrated manufacturer-controller-deployer stack.
- **GDPR ↔ DORA** — COMPLEMENTARY (SAME) (DORA exceeds GDPR): DORA's four-factor proportionality test (Art. 28(1) — nature, scale, complexity, importance) is a **sharper operationalisation** of the GDPR `sufficient guarantees` Art. 28(1) test. A financial entity already subject to DORA Art. 28 satisfies GDPR Art. 28(1) `sufficient guarantees` via the proportionality documentation, but **must explicitly document the GDPR-specific processor guarantees** that are not in the DORA Art. 30(2) closed list verbatim — instruction-bound processing per Art. 28(3)(a), audit rights per Art. 28(3)(h), sub-processor authorisation per Art. 28(2) — because DORA's ICT third-party risk regime is broader than GDPR's processor regime and does not separate the data-processing-counterparty dimension. The DORA four-factor test operationalises GDPR's qualitative `sufficient guarantees` threshold with sharper factors; there is **no contradiction** because the two thresholds are calibrated at different specificity — qualitative vs four-factor — and the DORA exceeds GDPR rather than contradicts it.
- **NIS2 ↔ CRA** — DIFFERENT-PERSPECTIVE (SAME): NIS 2 supplier relationships are at the *entity* level (the deployer's supplier list under Art. 21(2)(d) + Art. 21(3) supplier assessment three-prong: vulnerabilities + quality + secure-development procedures); CRA third-party component due diligence is at the *product* level (the manufacturer's component list under Art. 13(5)). The two lists **overlap when the deployer is the entity and the manufacturer supplies a CRA-compliant product** — the deployer does NIS 2-level supplier due diligence on the manufacturer (entity-to-entity), while the manufacturer does CRA-level component due diligence on its third-party components (product-internal). The objects and triggers are different (entity-level supplier / product-level component), so the obligations **layer vertically** rather than contradict. The `due diligence` POLY-S3 ambiguity in CRA Art. 13(5) (R1 procedural-only / R2 substantive-only / R3 procedural+substantive) is resolved by `SR-CRA-050` to R3 (procedural + substantive), which aligns with the NIS 2 supplier-assessment three-prong.
- **NIS2 ↔ DORA** — EQUAL (SAME) (DORA exceeds NIS 2): DORA's Art. 28(1) four-factor proportionality test (nature, scale, complexity, importance) operationalises the NIS 2 Art. 21(1) `appropriate and proportionate` chapeau with **explicit factors** — same object (supplier risk), same addressee (entity), but DORA's sharper test exceeds NIS 2's general test. A financial entity implementing DORA **exceeds** NIS 2's supply-chain security requirement in the financial-services subset; NIS 2 remains the floor for the broader essential / important-entity population. The label is correct: same object, same addressee, DORA's sharper test exceeds NIS 2's general test. There is **no contradiction** — the two thresholds are calibrated at different specificity (qualitative chapeau with Member-State-transposition-supplied detail vs four-factor with explicit listed factors) and the DORA exceeds NIS 2 rather than contradicts it.
- **CRA ↔ DORA** — DIFFERENT-PERSPECTIVE (SAME): CRA third-party component due diligence is **product-level** (the manufacturer's third-party components under Art. 13(5)); DORA ICT third-party risk is **entity-level** (the financial entity's ICT service providers under Art. 28(1)). The two are at different levels of the supply chain: **CRA = upstream** (manufacturer's inputs); **DORA = midstream** (deployer's ICT providers). The pair is typically **scope-disjoint because distinct actors handle distinct objects** — the CRA-bound manufacturer is upstream of the DORA-bound financial entity's ICT providers. When a financial entity deploys a CRA-compliant product, the manufacturer (CRA-bound) and the deployer (DORA-bound) are typically **different parties**. There is **no contradiction** because the two regimes target different supply-chain layers with different objects, different actors, and different triggers. The obligations compose into a 2-layer record when both apply, with the CRA covering the product side and the DORA covering the entity side of the same supply chain.

The vendor-risk-assessment architecture is **legitimately diverse** — each regulator targets a **distinct target object** (processor as legal entity / direct supplier as relationship / third-party component as technical artefact / ICT third-party service provider as financial-entity counterparty) at a **distinct trigger** (per-processor engagement / continuous supply-chain security / per-component integration / continuous ICT third-party risk management under proportionality) with a **distinct obligation anchor** (GDPR `sufficient guarantees` qualitative + Art. 28(3) DPA + Art. 28(2) sub-processor authorisation; NIS 2 `appropriate and proportionate` chapeau + Art. 21(2)(d) `direct` scope-limiter + Art. 21(3) supplier-assessment three-prong; CRA `due diligence` procedural + substantive + Art. 13(6) component vulnerability reporting + Art. 3(48) FOSS inclusion; DORA Art. 28(1) four-factor AND + Art. 28(5) CIF escalator + Art. 30(2) 9-element closed list). None. The four regulations look at supply chain from four distinct perspectives (processor / supplier / component / ICT third-party) and are layered, not contradictory.

##### SR cross-validation
NIST CSF 2.0 anchoring is dominated by **GV.SC-02** (Suppliers prioritised by criticality) and **ID.AM-04** (Resources prioritised based on classification). ##### Downstream implication
Phase 2 obligation derivation must produce a 4-tier supplier/vendor risk assessment: (1) GDPR processor due diligence, (2) NIS2 direct supplier security, (3) CRA third-party component due diligence, (4) DORA ICT third-party risk under proportionality. Phase 3 case generation must validate the 4-tier assessment chain.

### 3. Verbatim OJ text — Art. 4–6

#### 6.1 Art. 4 propagation pattern — three distinct proportionality regimes

Art. 4 establishes **three distinct proportionality regimes** that operate simultaneously:

- **Art. 4(1) — Ch. II proportionality** (CL4-1): default for the ICT risk management framework.
- **Art. 4(2) — Ch. III, IV, V Sec. I proportionality** (CL4-2): default for incident reporting, testing, and third-party risk.
- **Art. 4(3) — CA-review proportionality** (CL4-3): meta-provision requiring CAs to consider proportionality when reviewing the framework.

The three regimes are **not symmetric**: Art. 4(1) is self-implementing (the entity applies it directly); Art. 4(2) defers to chapter-specific clauses (Art. 16 simplification, Art. 25(3) microenterprise exception); Art. 4(3) is addressed to CAs.

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`principle of proportionality`); §5.1 (`proportionate`).
- **Structural implication:** the proportionality overlay is **inherited by every Ch. II–V Sec. I clause** in v0.2. Every clause card in `02b`–`05a` carries `proportionality-status: Art. 4 applies`. The v0.2 convention was established in the pilot file (`01_DORA_Art3_Definitions.md` §2 note on Art. 4).
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** Art. 4's proportionality pattern mirrors **NIS 2 Art. 1(2)** (proportionality in scope) and **CRA Art. 1(2)** (proportionality for microenterprises and SMEs). DORA Art. 4 is more elaborate than either, providing explicit CA-review (CL4-3) that NIS 2 and CRA do not have.

### 3. Verbatim OJ text — Art. 28–30

#### 4.1 CL28-1 — Art. 28 chapeau + (1) + (2) — Umbrella principles + strategy

**Source locus:** Art. 28 chapeau, (1)(a)+(b), and (2).

> Financial entities shall manage ICT third-party risk as an integral component of ICT risk within their ICT risk management framework as referred to in Article 6(1) …. shall **at all times, remain fully responsible** …. implemented in light of the **principle of proportionality**, taking into account [nature, scale, complexity, importance] and [risks … criticality or importance] …. shall **adopt, and regularly review**, a **strategy on ICT third-party risk**, taking into account the **multi-vendor strategy** …. include a **policy on the use of ICT services supporting critical or important functions** …. **regularly review the risks identified** ….

**Instance 1 — VAG — S3 — `at all times, remain fully responsible` + `principle of proportionality` (consolidated)**

Two Berry-classic qualifiers in the chapeau + (1)(a)+(b). Three materially distinct readings of the responsibility/proportionality nexus:

- **R1:** Strict (full liability for compliance + Art. 4(2)-proportionality).
- **R2:** Functional (oversight responsibility + entity-defined proportionality).
- **R3:** Hybrid (strict compliance, functional proportionality per Art. 4(2)).

| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Strict (full + Art. 4(2)) | In-Directive: literal `fully responsible` + Art. 4(2) reference |
| R2 | Functional (oversight + entity) | None in OJ |
| R3 | Hybrid | In-Directive: literal `at all times` + `principle of proportionality` |

R1 vs R2 admit materially distinct compliance populations. S3.

*Berry anchor:* §5.1 (`fully`, `at all times`, `proportionality`).

**Instance 2 — POLY — S2 — `individual and at group level` + `multi-vendor strategy` (consolidated)**

Two scoping qualifiers across (1)(b)(ii) and (2). The group-level assessment scope and the multi-vendor construct are both undefined.

- **R1:** Both individual AND group level; multi-vendor = ≥2 CTPPs per function.
- **R2:** Individual + sub-consolidated/consolidated where relevant; multi-vendor = goal-based (switchable).

| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Strict (both levels + ≥2 CTPPs) | In-Directive: literal `and` |
| R2 | Hybrid (where relevant + switchable) | In-Directive: literal `where relevant` + Art. 28(8) exit |

R2 is the literal reading (per `where relevant`). S2.

*Berry anchor:* §5.4.7 (AND); §5.1 (`multi-vendor`).

**Definitional anchor:** DORA Art. 3(18) `ICT third-party risk`; DORA Art. 3(19) `ICT third-party service provider`; DORA Art. 3(22) `critical or important function` (S3 tautology).
**Proportionality-status:** Art. 4 applies (explicit in Art. 28(1)(b) chapeau + limited exception for Art. 16(1) entities and microenterprises in Art. 28(2)).

#### 6.2 Art. 28 umbrella — five governance blocks

Art. 28 is the **management-body-facing umbrella clause**. Its 10 paragraphs split into five operational blocks (mapped to CL28-1 through CL28-5):

| Block | Paragraphs | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Principles + strategy | chapeau + (1) + (2) | Integral component, full responsibility, proportionality, multi-vendor, mgmt body review |
| Register | (3) | Documentation + reporting to CA |
| Pre-contract | (4)(a)–(e) | 5 sub-steps before entering arrangement |
| Security + audit | (5) + (6) | Information security standards + audit + auditor competence |
| Termination + exit | (7) + (8) | 4 termination grounds + exit strategies for critical/important |
| RTS/ITS | (9) + (10) | ESAs standards (templates + policy content) |

Each block is a governance checkpoint. **Art. 28 is the longest single article in DORA** (10 paragraphs) and the most operationally consequential. Block (4) explicitly cross-references **Art. 29** for concentration risk (CL28-3 → CL29-1 / CL29-2).
