##### Pair: GDPR ↔ NIS2

**GDPR article (verbatim, Art. 25(1)):**
> "Taking into account the state of the art, the cost of implementation and the nature, scope, context and purposes of processing as well as the risks of varying likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller shall … implement appropriate technical and organisational measures, such as pseudonymisation, which are designed to implement data-protection principles, such as data minimisation, in an effective manner and to integrate the necessary safeguards into the processing in order to meet the requirements of this Regulation and protect the rights of data subjects." — Art. 25(1) GDPR

**NIS2 article (verbatim, Art. 21(2)(e)):**
> "[Member States shall ensure that essential and important entities take appropriate and proportionate technical, operational and organisational measures to … ] (e) security in acquisition, development and maintenance of network and information systems, including vulnerability handling and disclosure." — Art. 21(2)(e) NIS 2

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: controller's means-determination and ongoing processing of personal data; data-protection properties (minimisation, pseudonymisation) integrated by design.
- NIS 2 scope: entity-level lifecycle security of network and information systems (NIS) across three coordinated phases: acquisition, development, maintenance.
- Scope overlap: **Y (when the same entity is both a controller and an NSIE processing personal data through NIS).**

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: controller's means-determination for personal-data processing.
- NIS 2 trigger: design / development / maintenance activity on NIS.
- Trigger alignment: **DIFFERENT** (data-protection focus vs. NIS-lifecycle focus) but co-applicable on the same product/system.

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: no quantitative threshold; proportionality is qualitative, anchored in `state of the art`, cost, nature, scope, context, purposes, risks.
- NIS 2: no quantitative threshold; Art. 21(1) appropriate-and-proportionate is qualitative, with Recital 56 anchoring proportionality factors.
- Tension: **NONE** (both qualitative proportionality).

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: supervisory authority (DPA), data subjects.
- NIS 2: management body, competent authority.

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: not codified; demonstrated via Art. 5(2) accountability (documented necessity assessment, often via DPIA).
- NIS 2: not codified for by-design content; entity-discretionary methodology under Recital 56.

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** COMPLEMENTARY
**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** **SAME — COMPLEMENTARY**
**Reasoning:** GDPR Art. 25(1) requires *what* must be designed in (data-protection properties via pseudonymisation and minimisation); NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(e) requires *that* secure lifecycle discipline applies across acquisition/development/maintenance. The two regimes stack at the same controller-NSIE entity, with the GDPR by-design outputs (pseudonymised-by-default pipelines) realised through the NIS 2 acquisition gate (supplier SDLC assessment) and development gate (in-house secure-by-design decisions per SR-NIS2-027). The OJ literal text does not contradict; the joint addressee discharges both obligations with distinct evidence trails.

**Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? **Y (when the controller-NSIE designs NIS that processes personal data).** No reclassification required.

**Downstream implication (Phase 1C Doc 07):** Phase 2 obligation derivation must produce a joint by-design stack: GDPR Art. 25(1) outputs (data-protection properties) become inputs to the NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(e) lifecycle evidence trail.

#### Validated relationships (from CRDA-deep §D-07.1)

Of 10 pairs, **10 verified as SAME — COMPLEMENTARY (all CRDA initial classifications held after OJ-text analysis): no CORRECTED, no SCOPE-DISJOINT in baseline, no CONTRADICTORY**. The five regulations look at secure-by-design from five distinct regulatory perspectives and are layered rather than contradictory:

- **GDPR ↔ NIS2** — COMPLEMENTARY (SAME): GDPR Art. 25(1) addresses *what* must be designed in (data-protection properties via pseudonymisation and minimisation); NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(e) addresses *that* secure lifecycle discipline applies across acquisition/development/maintenance. The two regimes stack at the same controller-NSIE entity, with the GDPR by-design outputs (pseudonymised-by-default pipelines) realised through the NIS 2 acquisition gate (supplier SDLC assessment) and development gate (in-house secure-by-design decisions per SR-NIS2-027). The OJ literal text does not contradict; the joint addressee discharges both obligations with distinct evidence trails.
- **GDPR ↔ CRA** — COMPLEMENTARY (SAME): GDPR Art. 25(1) addresses *what* data-protection properties must be designed in; CRA Art. 13(1)/(2) addresses *that* the design discipline applies to a product with digital elements across three coordinated verbs (designed, developed, produced) with risk-assessment propagation across 6 phases. For a CRA-regulated product that processes personal data, both regimes apply: the manufacturer's CRA design discipline discharges the engineering substrate, and the controller's GDPR Art. 25(1) discharges the personal-data overlay. The two converge on "design as a regulatory event" but address different design-output objects (data-protection properties vs. product cybersecurity).
- **GDPR ↔ DORA** — COMPLEMENTARY (SAME): DORA's Art. 9(4)(b) is not a dedicated by-design mandate — secure-by-design is operationalised through change management (Art. 9(4)(e)) and testing programme (Art. 24). The OJ literal text does not contain a dedicated by-design anchor; the SR-DORA-004 mapping to D-07.1 is partial. For a DORA financial entity that is a controller processing personal data, GDPR Art. 25(1) provides the data-protection by-design overlay while DORA's change-management six-step provides the engineering discipline. The two stack with distinct evidence trails (documented Art. 25(1) assessment on the controller side; documented Art. 6(1) ICT risk-management framework + Art. 9(4)(e) change-management + Art. 24 testing programme on the financial entity side).
- **GDPR ↔ AI_Act** — COMPLEMENTARY (SAME): GDPR Art. 25(1) addresses data-protection by-design; AI Act Art. 15(1) + Art. 17(1)(c) addresses high-risk AI system design (accuracy/robustness/cybersecurity) and the QMS design-control sub-process. AI-Act-Recital 78 explicitly preserves the GDPR. The two obligations stack — a high-risk AI system processing personal data discharges both with distinct design outputs (privacy property vs. AI-system property).
- **NIS2 ↔ CRA** — COMPLEMENTARY (SAME): NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(e) governs the entity's procurement and internal development of NIS (the user side); CRA Art. 13(1)/(2) governs the manufacturer's design of products (the maker side). The two regimes meet at the supply-chain boundary — NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(d) supplier obligations reference CRA-conformant products via SR-NIS2-020/024. No contradiction; full coverage requires both (entity-level supplier due diligence stacks with product-level by-design at the supplier-customer boundary).
- **NIS2 ↔ DORA** — COMPLEMENTARY (SAME): NIS 2 mandates lifecycle security as a continuous obligation; DORA mandates the change-management six-step (recorded/tested/assessed/approved/implemented/verified) per Art. 9(4)(e) that operationalises the maintenance phase. NIS 2 financial-entity coverage is light in this domain (financial entities under DORA are NSIEs partially, but DORA's dedicated obligations dominate). The two regimes meet at the maintenance phase: NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(e) maintenance-phase security requirements are operationalised through DORA Art. 9(4)(e) six-step process.
- **NIS2 ↔ AI_Act** — COMPLEMENTARY (SAME): NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(e) development-time obligations apply to NIS that embed AI; AI Act Art. 15(1) + Art. 17(1)(c) design obligations apply to high-risk AI systems regardless of NIS status. The regimes stack — NIS 2 procurement gate (SR-NIS2-026) and AI Act QMS (SR-AIACT-017) both apply to the same entity, with the AI Act design verification record supplementing the NIS 2 supplier-assessment evidence.
- **CRA ↔ DORA** — COMPLEMENTARY (SAME): CRA governs the products DORA entities procure and deploy; DORA's secure-by-design absence (no dedicated by-design mandate in OJ) is filled by the upstream CRA obligation. A DORA entity satisfies its design-procurement baseline by selecting CRA-conformant products and recording the conformance in its supplier-risk register per SR-DORA-006 (network/infrastructure) and SR-DORA-023 (change-management).
- **CRA ↔ AI_Act** — COMPLEMENTARY (SAME): For an AI-enabled product (CRA-regulated product that embeds a high-risk AI system), both regimes apply. CRA Art. 13(4) sentence 2 explicitly permits a "unified risk assessment" under AI Act Art. 9 to substitute for the CRA risk assessment when both apply — see SR-CRA-066 ambiguity_notes. The AI Act QMS and CRA Annex I conformity stack with the explicit cross-regulation consolidation rule for risk assessment.
- **DORA ↔ AI_Act** — COMPLEMENTARY (SAME): DORA financial entities deploying high-risk AI must satisfy both. DORA's change-management six-step (Art. 9(4)(e)) is the operational gate; AI Act QMS design controls (Art. 17(1)(c)) is the governance gate. The two do not overlap substantively; they stack (operational gate + governance gate).

The secure-by-design architecture is **legitimately diverse** — each regulator targets a **distinct target object** (data-protection-properties / NIS-lifecycle security / product-cybersecurity via Annex I Part I / ICT-infrastructure-management + change-mgmt / AI-system-accuracy-robustness-cybersecurity + QMS) at a **distinct trigger** (controller means-determination / NSIE NIS acquisition+development+maintenance / manufacturer places product on market / financial entity establishes ICT infrastructure / provider places high-risk AI system on market) with a **distinct obligation anchor** (GDPR Art. 25(1)+(2) `designed to implement data-protection principles` + state-of-the-art + 4-factor qualitative proportionality; NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(e) `security in acquisition, development and maintenance` + Art. 21(1) `appropriate and proportionate` chapeau + state-of-the-art floor; CRA Art. 13(1)+(2) `designed, developed and produced` per Annex I Part I + 6-phase risk-assessment propagation per Art. 13(2) + Art. 13(4) sentence 2 AI-Act-substitution; DORA Art. 9(4)(b) network/infrastructure-management + Art. 9(4)(e) change-management six-step + Art. 24 testing programme + Art. 9(4)(f) patch policies — **no dedicated by-design mandate**, by-design is implicit; AI Act Art. 15(1) accuracy/robustness/cybersecurity with lifecycle consistency + Art. 15(4)–(5) AI-attack-defence + Art. 17(1) QMS with 13 enumerated aspects including Art. 17(1)(c) design/design control/design verification + Recital 78 GDPR preservation). ##### Emergent tensions (5 regulations)
<!-- emergent: GDPR,NIS2,CRA,DORA,AI_Act -->

- **No by-design mandate in DORA creates a supplier-conformance gap**: All other 4 regulations impose a by-design obligation (GDPR via Art. 25; NIS 2 via Art. 21(2)(e); CRA via Art. 13(1)/(2); AI Act via Art. 17(1)). DORA's coverage is implicit (operationalised through change-management). A DORA-only entity has no OJ-mandated by-design baseline, and DORA entities procuring CRA-regulated products must demonstrate that the upstream CRA design discipline substitutes for the absent DORA by-design obligation.
- **The `state of the art` anchor is duplicated but uncoordinated**: GDPR Art. 25(1), NIS 2 Art. 21(1) sentence 2, CRA Annex I opening, DORA Art. 4, and AI Act Art. 9(1) all anchor to `state of the art` — but each regulation interprets the anchor independently. An entity implementing all 5 must reconcile the five `state of the art` baselines (industry-framework reading in GDPR; cost/state-of-the-art in NIS 2; Annex I essential-requirements in CRA; EBA Guidelines in DORA; harmonised standards + AI Act Art. 9 risk-management in AI Act).

<!-- /emergent -->

##### SR cross-validation
The 5 regulations map D-07.1 by-design to the NIST CSF 2.0 Subcategories **PR.PS-06** (Secure software development practices integrated, and their performance is monitored throughout the SDLC) and **PR.PS-02** (Software is maintained, replaced, and removed commensurate with risk) as the dominant pair. GDPR additionally maps **PR.DS-12** (Data managed consistent with risk strategy) for the by-design data-protection dimension; CRA additionally maps **PR.PS-01** (Configuration management practices established, documented, and applied) for the by-default layer. The CSF mapping converges on the SDLC-discipline anchor with regulation-specific additions on the data/configuration/asset dimensions.

##### Downstream implication
A Phase 1 entity must implement at minimum one by-design obligation from each applicable regulation — the regimes stack, not substitute. The CRA-conformant product design serves as the upstream gate for NIS 2 / DORA / AI Act deployment decisions. GDPR by-design outputs (pseudonymisation, minimisation) become a CRA Annex I input only when the product processes personal data — the cross-regulation propagation is conditional on data-classification overlap.

##### Pair: GDPR ↔ NIS2

**GDPR article (verbatim, Art. 25(1)):**
> "Taking into account the state of the art, the cost of implementation and the nature, scope, context and purposes of processing as well as the risks of varying likelihood and severity for the rights and freedoms of natural persons, the controller shall … implement appropriate technical and organisational measures, such as pseudonymisation, which are designed to implement data-protection principles, such as data minimisation, in an effective manner and to integrate the necessary safeguards into the processing in order to meet the requirements of this Regulation and protect the rights of data subjects." — Art. 25(1) GDPR

**NIS2 article (verbatim, Art. 21(2)(e)):**
> "[Member States shall ensure that essential and important entities take appropriate and proportionate technical, operational and organisational measures to … ] (e) security in acquisition, development and maintenance of network and information systems, including vulnerability handling and disclosure." — Art. 21(2)(e) NIS 2

**Scope comparison:**
- GDPR scope: controller's means-determination and ongoing processing of personal data; data-protection properties (minimisation, pseudonymisation) integrated by design.
- NIS 2 scope: entity-level lifecycle security of network and information systems (NIS) across three coordinated phases: acquisition, development, maintenance.
- Scope overlap: **Y (when the same entity is both a controller and an NSIE processing personal data through NIS).**

**Trigger comparison:**
- GDPR trigger: controller's means-determination for personal-data processing.
- NIS 2 trigger: design / development / maintenance activity on NIS.
- Trigger alignment: **DIFFERENT** (data-protection focus vs. NIS-lifecycle focus) but co-applicable on the same product/system.

**Threshold/timeline comparison:**
- GDPR: no quantitative threshold; proportionality is qualitative, anchored in `state of the art`, cost, nature, scope, context, purposes, risks.
- NIS 2: no quantitative threshold; Art. 21(1) appropriate-and-proportionate is qualitative, with Recital 56 anchoring proportionality factors.
- Tension: **NONE** (both qualitative proportionality).

**Recipient comparison:**
- GDPR: supervisory authority (DPA), data subjects.
- NIS 2: management body, competent authority.

**Content template comparison:**
- GDPR: not codified; demonstrated via Art. 5(2) accountability (documented necessity assessment, often via DPIA).
- NIS 2: not codified for by-design content; entity-discretionary methodology under Recital 56.

**Classified relationship (from CRDA):** COMPLEMENTARY
**Verified relationship (after OJ-text analysis):** **SAME — COMPLEMENTARY**
**Reasoning:** GDPR Art. 25(1) requires *what* must be designed in (data-protection properties via pseudonymisation and minimisation); NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(e) requires *that* secure lifecycle discipline applies across acquisition/development/maintenance. The two regimes stack at the same controller-NSIE entity, with the GDPR by-design outputs (pseudonymised-by-default pipelines) realised through the NIS 2 acquisition gate (supplier SDLC assessment) and development gate (in-house secure-by-design decisions per SR-NIS2-027). **Scope-disjoint test:** Does this pair actually overlap on a single event at a single party? **Y (when the controller-NSIE designs NIS that processes personal data).** No reclassification required.

**Downstream implication (Phase 1C Doc 07):** Phase 2 obligation derivation must produce a joint by-design stack: GDPR Art. 25(1) outputs (data-protection properties) become inputs to the NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(e) lifecycle evidence trail.

##### Art. 5(1)(f) — Integrity and confidentiality

**Clause: GDPR-CL06** | type: principle | obligatedParty: CONTROLLER | obligationType: CONTINUOUS

**Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`appropriate`), §5.4.7 (`or` coordination).

**Instance 1 — VAG / S3 / `appropriate security`, `appropriate technical or organisational measures`**

`appropriate` is the recurring Berry-flagged term. `appropriate technical or organisational measures` uses OR whose inclusivity is unspecified. Art. 32 (security of processing) anchors `appropriate` to a risk-based test but does not resolve the OR.

**Variant readings:**
| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | `or` is inclusive: at least one of technical OR organisational measures required. | Strict grammatical reading. |
| R2 | `or` is inclusive: both required in combination (despite `or`). | Industry reading; ISO 27001 control set interpretation. |
| R3 | `or` is exclusive: the controller chooses which category. | Loose reading. |

R1 vs R2 yield materially distinct compliance obligations (R1 permits policy-only compliance for low-tech processing; R2 requires both).

**Instance 2 — COORD / S3 / `unauthorised or unlawful processing`, `accidental loss, destruction or damage`**

Two OR coordinations enumerating breach types. The 5-fold enumeration (unauthorised / unlawful / loss / destruction / damage) is similar to Art. 4(12) `personal data breach` but not identical (Art. 4(12) adds `alteration`, `disclosure of`, `access to`).

**Variant readings:**
| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Any of the 5 events triggers `appropriate security` obligations. | Strict text. |
| R2 | The 5 events are illustrative; the controller judges which events trigger obligations. | Loose reading. |

R1 vs R2 yield materially distinct compliance obligations on breach response.

### 1. Article 13 — full text (key paragraphs)

> **Article 13 — Obligations of manufacturers**
>
> **1.** When placing a product with digital elements on the market, manufacturers shall ensure that it has been designed, developed and produced in accordance with the essential cybersecurity requirements set out in Part I of Annex I.

> **2.** For the purpose of complying with paragraph 1, manufacturers shall undertake an assessment of the cybersecurity risks associated with a product with digital elements and take the outcome of that assessment into account during the **planning, design, development, production, delivery and maintenance phases** of the product with digital elements with a view to minimising cybersecurity risks, preventing incidents and minimising their impact, **including in relation to the health and safety of users**.

> **3.** The cybersecurity risk assessment shall be documented and updated as appropriate during a support period to be determined in accordance with paragraph 8 of this Article. That cybersecurity risk assessment shall comprise at least an analysis of cybersecurity risks based on the **intended purpose and reasonably foreseeable use, as well as the conditions of use, of the product with digital elements**, such as the operational environment or the assets to be protected, taking into account the length of time the product is expected to be in use. The cybersecurity risk assessment shall indicate whether and, if so in what manner, the security requirements set out in Part I, point (2), of Annex I are applicable to the relevant product with digital elements and how those requirements are implemented as informed by the cybersecurity risk assessment. ### 1. Annex I — full text

#### Part II — Vulnerability handling requirements

> Manufacturers of products with digital elements shall:
>
> **(1)** identify and document vulnerabilities and components contained in products with digital elements, including by drawing up a **software bill of materials** in a commonly used and machine-readable format covering at the very least the **top-level dependencies** of the products;
>
> **(2)** in relation to the risks posed to products with digital elements, **address and remediate vulnerabilities without delay**, including by providing security updates; where technically feasible, new security updates shall be provided **separately from functionality updates**;
>
> **(3)** apply **effective and regular tests and reviews** of the security of the product with digital elements;
>
> **(4)** once a security update has been made available, **share and publicly disclose** information about fixed vulnerabilities, including a description of the vulnerabilities, information allowing users to identify the product with digital elements affected, the impacts of the vulnerabilities, their severity and clear and accessible information helping users to remediate the vulnerabilities; in duly justified cases, where manufacturers consider the security risks of publication to outweigh the security benefits, they may **delay making public information** regarding a fixed vulnerability until after users have been given the possibility to apply the relevant patch;
>
> **(5)** put in place and enforce a **policy on coordinated vulnerability disclosure**;
>

**Source locus:**

#### 6.1 Art. 25(1) — 12-modality open list (POLY/S3)

Art. 25(1) enumerates 12 specific test modalities under the `such as …` open-list signal. The structural pattern is **identical to CRA Annex I Part I §2(d)** (`authentication, identity or access management systems` under `including but not limited to`) and to the CRA Annex I enumeration generally.

- **Cross-regulation consistency:** CRA, NIS 2 (Art. 21(2)(e) `secure system development and maintenance` includes similar open enumerations), and DORA all converge on the open-list pattern. This is a **regulatory-drafting convention**, not an artefact of any single regulation.

#### 6.5 Art. 25(2) — Sectoral carve-out for CSD/CCP

Art. 25(2) imposes a **pre-deployment vulnerability assessment obligation on two specific entity types** (CSDs and CCPs). This is a sectoral carve-out unique in Ch. IV — no other clause names specific entity types.

- **Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`central securities depositories`, `central counterparties`).
- **Structural implication:** CSDs and CCPs are **financial market infrastructures (FMIs)** with systemic importance. The pre-deployment assessment obligation reflects the FMI-specific risk profile (a deployment-induced vulnerability could cascade to the entire clearing or settlement system).
- **Cross-regulation consistency:** CRA does not impose pre-deployment obligations. NIS 2 Art. 21(2)(f) requires `policies and procedures for cryptography` but not pre-deployment assessments. **DORA Art. 25(2) is a sectoral specificity.**

#### 2.14 AIA-C14 — Art. 14(1) Human oversight design

**Source locus:** `High-risk AI systems shall be designed and developed in such a way, including with appropriate human-machine interface tools, that they can be effectively overseen by natural persons during the period in which they are in use`.

* **VAG — S2** — `appropriate`, `effectively overseen`.
* **POLY — S2** — `effectively overseen` — supervisor-can-intervene vs. supervisor-does-intervene vs. supervisor-understands. The three senses drive different design choices (stop-button vs. dashboard vs. explanation-panel).

**Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`appropriate`, `effectively`), §3.3.1 (`overseen`).

#### 2.16 AIA-C16 — Art. 15(1) Accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity

**Source locus:** `High-risk AI systems shall be designed and developed in such a way that they achieve an appropriate level of accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity, and that they perform consistently in those respects throughout their lifecycle`.

* **VAG — S3** — `appropriate level of accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity`. The three AI-technical properties are each inquiry-resistant: `accuracy` (against which metric? at what threshold?), `robustness` (against which perturbations?), `cybersecurity` (against which threat model?).
* **POLY — S3** — `accuracy` (precision vs. recall vs. F1 vs. calibration), `robustness` (distributional shift vs. adversarial vs. operational), `cybersecurity` (cf. CRA C07 — undefined at the EU level).
* **COORD — S2** — `accuracy, robustness AND cybersecurity` (3-way AND of distinct properties). Partial compliance (2 of 3) is undefined.

**Berry anchor:** §5.1 (`appropriate level`), §3.3.1 (`accuracy`, `robustness`, `cybersecurity`), §5.4.7.

#### 2.21 AIA-C21 — Art. 17(1)(c) Design control

**Source locus:** `techniques, procedures and systematic actions to be used for the design, design control and design verification of the high-risk AI system`.

* **POLY — S2** — `design` vs. `design control` vs. `design verification` — three engineering terms with overlapping but distinct industry meanings.
* **COORD — S2** — `design, design control AND design verification` (3-way AND of near-synonyms); `techniques, procedures AND systematic actions` (3-way AND of artefact types).

**Berry anchor:** §3.3.1 (`design`/`control`/`verification`), §5.5 (twin 3-way AND).
